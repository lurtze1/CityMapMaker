# Research: water and infrastructure in pre-industrial towns

Date: 2026-10-20. Method: web search, then fetch of each cited page. A claim marked
"(unverified)" came from a search-result snippet only; the page itself was not fetched or
could not be read. Most fetched sources are encyclopaedic or heritage-record pages; few are
peer-reviewed papers. Where a scholarly paper is cited, usually only its abstract was readable.

## Water and infrastructure

### Findings

**Water supply: wells, conduits, fountains**

- English towns. Piped water systems run by town councils were more common than historians
  once thought. They grew in the later Middle Ages, paid for by charity and run by civic
  authorities. In the 1540s John Leland saw conduits even in small towns. A *conduit* is a
  public outlet (often a small stone building) fed by a pipe from a spring.
  (Lee 2014, abstract, [S1])
- Backyard wells. Excavations in English towns find wells, cesspits and rubbish pits behind
  the street-front houses, in the back yards of each plot (unverified, search snippet for
  Huntingdon and Bury St Edmunds; the PDF [S28] could not be read).
- Roman towns. The aqueduct ends at a *castellum divisorium*, a distribution tank. At Nîmes,
  ten lead pipes leave the basin and carry water to public buildings, fountains and private
  houses [S2]. The Nîmes page does not say the tank is on the highest point; that point is
  Mont Cavalier with the Tour Magne [S2].
- Pompeii. A 2023 study measured walking routes from every house door to every public
  fountain. Most residents had good access, but access was clearly worse at the edges of the
  system [S3]. The count of 42 public fountains is from a search snippet (unverified).
- Islamic towns: the *sabil*, a free public drinking-water kiosk built over an underground
  cistern. Sabils were built at crossroads, in squares and outside mosques. In Mamluk Cairo
  they were nearly always on a corner of a larger religious building facing a busy street or
  junction. Free-standing sabils became common only under Ottoman rule after 1517. In
  Istanbul they often stood at busy junctions or street corners [S4].
- Persian towns: the *qanat*, a gently sloping tunnel that brings groundwater from a hillside
  to the surface by gravity. Its water "define[s] both the social regions in the city and the
  layout of the city". Below the outlet, water runs in surface channels called *jubs*, and
  "the streets normally parallel the jubs". Wealthier households sit near the outlet, where
  the water is cleanest [S5].
- Persian *ab anbar*, a covered underground drinking-water cistern fed by a qanat. Some were
  built under caravanserais or mosques, and one in Qazvin is in the bazaar. Hot cities (Yazd,
  Kashan) cool them with windcatchers, tall towers that draw air down [S6]. The claim that
  Qazvin had 151 public ab anbars around 1900 is unverified (search snippet).
- Indian *stepwell*, a well reached by flights of steps down to the water. Stepwells sat
  either in a temple complex (in front of or opposite its wall) or on the edge of a village.
  Stepped ponds usually go with temples. Bundi in Rajasthan has over 60 stepwells in and
  around the town; Delhi has 32. Ashokan inscriptions record wells with steps at intervals of
  8 *kos* (about 33.5 km) along roads [S7].
- Chinese towns (Beijing). An oral history recalls more than 700 wells in the inner city and
  more than 500 in the outer city, and more than 80 lanes named after wells. In the Yuan
  capital people "built their houses around the wells, and the passages between courtyards
  became hutongs (lanes)". Water carriers delivered from wells to homes [S8]. This is one
  man's memory, not a census.
- Mesoamerica (Tenochtitlan). Three main causeways linked the island city to the shore, cut
  by removable bridges so canoes could pass. Canals reached every district. Two terracotta
  aqueducts over 4 km long brought spring water from Chapultepec. A dike 12 to 16 km long
  (about 1453) held brackish lake water away from the city. Chinampas, farm plots built up
  from lake mud, surrounded the raised causeways [S9].

**Mills**

- England had over 6,000 mills in the Domesday survey of 1086, about one per 40 households,
  most of them water mills for grain. Mills often shared weirs with fisheries. Water was
  taken off rivers in man-made channels (Abingdon, about 960; Old Windsor). Windmills appear
  about a century after 1066 [S10]. The commonly quoted figure 5,624 is unverified.
- Colchester had eight mills for most of the 13th and 14th centuries: five on the river
  Colne and three on a small stream south of the town. They sat at bridges (East mill at
  East bridge, Hythe mill just above Hythe bridge, North mill near North bridge) and just
  outside a gate (Middle mill outside Ryegate). Mills so close together ran short of water,
  so owners raised the millponds, which then overflowed and flooded meadows and roads in
  1407, 1410, 1411, 1414 and 1429. Hythe mill was pulled down because it blocked boats. In
  1702 seven windmills stood south-west and south-east of the walls [S11].
- Dulverton (Exmoor). A *leat* (also *mill race*: a man-made channel feeding a mill) leaves
  the river at a 65 m weir north of the town, runs over half a kilometre through the town,
  drives four mills and rejoins the river. The survey concluded the leat is older than the
  town buildings: it follows the contours, so the streets were laid out around it [S12].
- Tide mills are "usually situated in river estuaries". A dam with a sluice closes a tidal
  inlet; the pond fills at high tide through a one-way gate and drives the wheel at low tide.
  About 200 are documented in the British Isles; London had about 76 in the 18th century,
  two of them on London Bridge [S13].
- Windmills on walls. Leiden had nineteen windmills on its ramparts [S14]. Amsterdam's De
  Bloem (1768) stood on a bastion of the city wall, and Bruges had windmills on its ramparts
  from the 13th century (both unverified, search snippets).
- English medieval windmills were *post mills* (the whole body turns on one post) set on
  earth mounds 15 to 25 m across, often on hill brows or reused older mounds (unverified,
  Historic England search snippets).

**Bridges and fords**

- Many towns grew at a ford and kept it after a bridge was built; Bradford-on-Avon's ford
  carried heavy traffic into the late 19th century (unverified, search snippet).
- Old London Bridge: about 130 rented properties on it by 1460, a fortified gate tower at the
  Southwark end and a drawbridge tower on the north side [S15]. Its chapel stood on the 11th
  of 19 piers, near the middle [S16].
- Bridge chapels were built into the bridge or on land at its end. Purposes: prayers for
  benefactors and travellers, and sometimes toll collection. Surviving English examples are
  on the bridge (Rotherham, Wakefield, St Ives) or at the end (Derby, Rochester) [S17]. A 2010
  survey counted at least 92 English bridges with chapels and at least 18 fortified urban
  bridges (unverified; quoted second-hand in a search snippet).
- Edo (Tokyo). Ryogoku bridge was built in 1660 after people trapped on the riverbank died in
  the 1657 fire. Open squares (*hirokoji*, "broad alley") were made at both ends as
  firebreaks, and they became busy market quarters [S18].
- Ferries: no sourced placement rule found.

**Harbours and quays**

- London's Legal Quays (1559): goods could be landed only at twenty named quays, together
  1,419 ft (433 m) of frontage, all on the north bank between London Bridge and the Tower,
  under customs officers. Custom House Quay was one of them [S19].
- After the 1666 fire, no building could stand within 40 ft of the Thames quays and wharves
  between the Tower and the Temple Stairs, except cranes and sheds; Thames Street was raised
  by at least 3 ft [S20].
- Newcastle: the town wall ran between the town and the quay, with several water gates for
  loading ships. In 1616 they were shut at night. The quay wall was demolished in 1763 as a
  hindrance to business [S21].
- Gdańsk: the great port crane (1442-44) is built into the city wall as a water gate on the
  river Motława [S22].
- Venice: the Arsenal shipyard was walled, about 45 ha (about 15% of the city), with nearly
  16,000 workers in the early 16th century [S23].
- Viking-age trading towns (Hedeby, Birka): jetties ran out from the shore, and the jetties
  themselves may have been the marketplace; Hedeby seems to have had no market square
  (unverified, UNESCO page returned 403; search snippets only).

**Drainage and sanitation**

- London: public latrines were often built on bridges so waste fell into the river; the
  Thames, Walbrook and Fleet are named. By the 15th century there were over a dozen. In 1382
  London Bridge's wardens spent £11 on one [S24].
- The Fleet ran just west of the city wall and "became a gutter for latrines, slaughterhouses,
  and tanneries". In 1343 butchers were told to dump waste there [S25].
- Early modern London: gutters ran down the middle or sides of streets; rakers carried rubbish
  to dumping zones "in and around the city"; cesspools could only be emptied at night [S26].
  The 13th-century rule of 2.5 ft between a stone-lined cesspit and the neighbour's land is
  unverified.
- Fire rules after London's 1666 fire: outer walls of brick or stone; four classes of house,
  each tied to the class of street it faces (by-lane: 2 storeys; street of note: 3; high
  street: 4; mansions at most 4); party-wall thickness set per class [S20].
- Edo after 1657: blocks were divided by wide firebreak streets (*hiyokechi*, *hirokoji*);
  every block had to build a fire-watch tower [S27].

**Defence-related water**

- Newcastle's outer ditch, the King's Dyke, was over 11 m wide and 4.5 m deep, finished by
  1317. The wall had six main gates and seventeen towers [S21].
- Town ditches filled by the tide (Bridgwater) or by a diverted stream (Tewkesbury)
  (unverified, search snippets).

### What this means for a generator

- Give water outlets their own placement rule: public fountain or sabil at junctions and
  squares (rule 1), private wells as a back-plot feature that a city overview map does not
  need to draw (rule 2). Density per population cannot be sourced; make it a spec value
  with no default, or a GM? question.
- Mills belong to the river, not the town grid: place water mills at bridges, just outside
  gates or on a leat drawn off the river by a weir (rules 10-12). A leat is a new linear
  water feature in the spec, and the strip beside a millpond is excluded from building.
- Windmills are an edge feature: outside the wall on high ground, or on bastions when the
  culture pack says so (rule 14). This is a rule-pack choice, not a universal.
- Bridge ends are special cells: a gate tower, an optional chapel, a bridgehead square or
  suburb on the far bank (rules 15-16). Houses on the bridge are an optional flag per bridge.
- Quays go on the town bank below the lowest bridge, with a strip kept free of houses
  (rule 17, rule 24's 40 ft); a customs house sits on a quay; a wall with water gates may
  separate quay and town (rule 18).
- Culture packs differ in kind, not only in numbers: qanat towns orient streets along water
  channels downhill (rule 5); lake cities use causeways and canals (rule 9); Indian towns
  place stepwells by temples and the settlement edge (rule 7). These map onto ADR-017's closed
  constraint kinds as "near", "along", "at edge", "at junction".
