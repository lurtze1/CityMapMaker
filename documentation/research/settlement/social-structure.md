# Research: who lives and works where in a pre-industrial town

Research date: 2026-10-20. Every source below was fetched in this session unless a line says
"(unverified)". Quotes are copied from the fetched page. In a few cases the fetch tool
summarised a long page, and the quote comes from that summary. Where I had only a search-engine
summary and not the page itself, the claim is marked "(unverified)".

## Social and spatial structure

Terms used below:

- **Pre-industrial city**: a town before factories and steam power, from ancient times to about 1800-1850.
- **Segregation**: groups living apart. Here it can be by wealth, religion, origin or trade.
- **Extramural**: outside the town wall. **Intramural** means inside it.
- **Hisba**: Islamic market law. A *hisba manual* is a handbook for the market inspector (*muhtasib*).
- **Suq / souk**: an Islamic market street or market district.
- **Faubourg**: a French word for a settlement that grew along a road outside a town gate.
- **Fang**: a walled ward (a block with its own wall and gates) in a Chinese capital such as Tang Chang'an.
- **Mellah**: the walled Jewish quarter of a Moroccan town.
- **Fondaco / kontor**: a walled compound where foreign merchants lived, stored goods and traded.
- **Leprosarium** (plural leprosaria): a hospital or refuge for people with leprosy.
- **Noxious trade**: a smelly, dirty or dangerous trade, such as tanning, slaughtering, pottery kilns or dyeing.

### Findings

**1. The Sjoberg model and its critics.** Gideon Sjoberg (*The Preindustrial City*, 1960)
said the political and religious elite lived in the centre. A Dutch summary of his pages 95-103
gives his three reasons: the elite lived near the public buildings where they held power, the
centre carried status from those buildings, and the centre was the safest place during an
attack. Status fell with distance from the centre, and the poor and outcast groups lived at the
edge, by the walls ([TSEG, Lesger, van Leeuwen and Vissers 2013](https://tseg.nl/article/download/7456/8086/11198)).

Critics:

- **Vance (1971)** said that from the 16th century the centre filled up with economic
  activity. People who could afford it left for the edge, where they had room for large houses
  (same TSEG article, paraphrased from the Dutch). Another summary says that in Vance's
  account "over time centre plots were filled to the brim with degraded housing for the lower
  classes" ([Härkönen 2024, DSH](https://academic.oup.com/dsh/article/39/2/532/7659183)).
- **Langton (1975)** studied 17th-century British cities ("Residential patterns in
  pre-industrial cities", *Transactions of the Institute of British Geographers* 65, 1-27,
  citation from the TSEG footnote). Härkönen summarises his Newcastle result: "the wealthy
  ruling elite of merchants neither resided in the periphery nor were members of a feudal
  elite". I did not read Langton's own text.
- **Lesger and van Leeuwen** (Alkmaar, Delft, Amsterdam, 16th-19th century) found a
  mosaic. The poor were mostly at the edge, as Sjoberg said. But the richest people in
  Amsterdam did not live by the big public buildings on the Dam. They lived in a quiet ring just
  outside the centre: the canal belt (*grachtengordel*). The busy centre was noisy and
  crowded, so the elite moved one step out. Inside every district there was also segregation
  "around the corner". The rich lived on main canals and main streets, middle groups on lesser
  canals and side streets, and the poorest in back courts reached by an alley
  ([TSEG 2013](https://tseg.nl/article/download/7456/8086/11198);
  [UvA abstract](https://dare.uva.nl/id/7a6c8c9c-cba3-4334-8186-5002aa09d876)). The
  2012 English article on the same cities says Amsterdam's merchant elite used political power
  to create a spacious residential area free of noisy and smelly trades (search summary of
  [JIH 42:3](https://direct.mit.edu/jinh/article-abstract/42/3/333/50389/Residential-Segregation-from-the-Sixteenth-to-the),
  unverified).
- **Wind (industrial era, not pre-industrial).** Heblich, Trew and Zylberberg (*Journal of
  Political Economy*, 2021) used about 5,000 factory chimneys in English cities. Prevailing
  winds blew smoke east, and the poor ended up on the east side. Their opening line is "Why are
  the east sides of formerly industrial cities often the more deprived?"
  ([St Andrews record](https://research-portal.st-andrews.ac.uk/en/publications/east-side-story-historical-pollution-and-persistent-neighborhood-/)).
  This is about coal smoke after 1800. I found no source that the rich chose the upwind
  side before industry.

**2. Noxious trades go to the edge and to water.** The pattern is strong. The reasons are smell,
water supply and space.

- Tanners needed running water. "Downstream, residents were assaulted by the horrific
  stenches." Tanners and skinners "were often kept outside of a city's walls". Bristol banned
  tanning "within the walls, to wit in the highways where the majority of the people pass".
  Siena's ordinances kept the smelliest steps "downstream from the public fountains". In London,
  the Walbrook stream by the tanners' quarter was so polluted it was "obstructed by dung"
  ([Medievalists.net](https://www.medievalists.net/?p=111561)).
- Dublin: a tannery used from the medieval period to the 17th century "located outside the
  city proper", "near the banks of the River Poddle". The street name Blackpitts is "almost
  certainly referring to the pits or vats of black tanning liquor"
  ([excavations.ie](https://excavations.ie/?p=21688)).
- London butchers. In 1371 a royal writ ordered a proclamation of "an ordinance forbidding the
  slaughtering of beasts within the City". A second writ said the ban did not apply to the
  butchers of Eastcheap and "les Stokkes"
  ([Letter-Book G, British History Online](https://www.british-history.ac.uk/node/26629)).
  So the law had exceptions for established central markets. A search summary says the 1380
  Parliament named Stratford-le-Bow and Knightsbridge as slaughter places, because of stench
  and offal in the Thames (unverified; I did not fetch that page).
- Al-Andalus (Islamic Spain). Hisba rules: animals could be slaughtered only in vats, and
  blood and tripe had to be carried outside. The fishmonger had to set up away from the street
  because of the smell. The tanners' quarter of Toledo lay south of the city, where it did not
  spoil the aqueduct supply. Toledo's kilns stood in the old Roman circus, away from homes.
  Seville's tile and brick makers were encouraged to work by the town ditch, where there was
  room. Trades that needed space and water settled by the river in thinly built areas
  ([OpenEdition, "La ville en mouvement"](https://books.openedition.org/pur/300752); the
  fetch tool read the first 100,000 characters only).
- **Ibn Abdun, Seville, late 11th or early 12th century.** His hisba treatise says potteries,
  "por la escasez de espacio libre intramuros, deben instalarse alrededor del foso fuera de la
  muralla" (because there is little free space inside the walls, they must be set up around
  the moat outside the wall). Garrido Amorós calls this the only written proof of extramural
  potteries in al-Andalus. It cannot be extended to every Islamic town. As towns grew,
  potteries moved further out "por dos razones: la contaminación y la necesidad de espacio"
  (pollution and need for space). Potteries are often found next to cemeteries, and some
  later became cemeteries
  ([Garrido Amorós 2019, Diacronía](https://ucm.es/data/cont/media/www/pag-127133/Garrido%20Amor%C3%B3s,%20P..pdf)).
  I did not find Ibn Abdun's own words on tanners.

**3. Clean trades near the temple, dirty trades near the gate (the "Islamic city" model).**
The Marçais brothers (French scholars, 1920s-40s) described a suq ordered from the Great
Mosque out to the gates. A search summary gives the order: sellers of candles, incense and
perfume, and booksellers and bookbinders by the mosque; textiles, jewellers and smiths
further out; caravan places and provision sellers by the gates; dyers, tanners and potters at
the edge (search summary of [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/works/100784839), page refused
fetch, unverified). A Brown University course page states the principle: "the 'cleanest'
trades are positioned closest to the mosque"
([Brown](https://webhelper.brown.edu/joukowsky/courses/afterpharaohs2010/13953.html)).
Janet Abu-Lughod (1987) criticised the whole "Islamic city" model as built from too few cases,
mostly in Morocco and Syria ([IJMES abstract and references](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/S0020743800031822)).
Fez does not fit neatly: its big tanneries are inside the old city (search summary, unverified).
Treat the model as a tendency.

**4. Trade streets.** In medieval London, guild members "set up shop in designated spots in
Cheapside", and "the trading influence of some of the larger guilds can be seen in the names of
the side streets": Ironmonger Lane, Milk Street, Bread Street, Wood Street, Poultry
([Fordham Medieval London](https://medievallondon.ace.fordham.edu/exhibits/show/medieval-london-sites/cheapside)).
Cheapside comes from *chepe*, "a place for trading". Tang Chang'an's East Market had an
"Ironmongers' Lane" ([Wikipedia, Chang'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27an)).

**5. Religious and foreign quarters.** Four placement patterns show up:

- **Next to the ruler, walled, for protection and control.** Speyer, 1084: Bishop Rüdiger
  wrote "I placed them outside the town and some way off from the houses of the rest of the
  citizens" and "I surrounded them with a wall", "lest they should be too easily disturbed by
  the insolence of the citizens" ([Fordham Sourcebook](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/source/1084landjews.asp)).
  Moroccan mellahs were "typically near the residence of the sultan or governor", next to the
  palace in Fez el-Jdid, partly for protection and partly to "assess taxes, and keep count of
  the community" ([Wikipedia, Mellah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mellah)).
- **Locked at night.** Venice, 29 March 1516: all Jews had to move into the Ghetto Nuovo,
  were "locked into the ghetto overnight", and paid for "round-the-clock surveillance of the
  gates" ([Wikipedia, Venetian Ghetto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venetian_Ghetto)).
- **Foreign merchants on the waterfront, in a walled compound.** London's Steelyard (Hanseatic
  League) lay "on the north bank of the Thames", "a separate walled community with its own
  warehouses on the river"; the kontor "required that Hansards lived on the Steelyard"
  ([Wikipedia, Steelyard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steelyard)). Venice's Fondaco dei
  Tedeschi stood on the Grand Canal by the Rialto Bridge, with storerooms reachable by water
  and lodgings above ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fondaco_dei_Tedeschi)).
  Nagasaki's Dejima was an artificial island "120 meters (390 ft) by 75 m (246 ft)", joined by
  "a small bridge, guarded on both sides"; Japanese civilians were banned from entering
  ([Wikipedia, Dejima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dejima)).
- **A traditional Christian quarter inside the wall, by a gate.** Damascus: "The area around
  Bāb Tūmā in the northeastern part of the old city has been the traditional Christian Quarter"
  ([GEDSH, Damascus](https://gedsh.bethmardutho.org/Damascus)).

**6. Caste and rank by direction (ancient India).** The *Arthashastra* (Kautilya, Book II,
Chapter IV, Shamasastry translation) gives a full plan. The palace is "to the north from the
centre", taking one-ninth of the site. Merchants in scents, garlands and grain, expert artisans
and Kshatriyas live on the east. City superintendents, sellers of cooked rice, liquor and meat,
prostitutes, musicians and Vaishyas on the south. Workers in thread, mats, skins and armour,
and Shudras on the west. The city deity, ironsmiths, jewel workers and Brahmans on the north.
"In the several corners, guilds and corporations of workmen shall reside." Gods' shrines stand
in the centre. "Either to the north or the east, burial or cremation grounds shall be
situated", and "Heretics and Chandalas shall live beyond the burial grounds". There is "a
water-well for every ten houses"
([archive.org text](https://archive.org/stream/kautilyasarthash00sham/kautilyasarthash00sham_djvu.txt)).
This is a prescriptive text (how a city should be), not a survey of a real one.

**7. Chinese ritual plan and ward curfew.** The *Kaogong ji* (in the *Rites of Zhou*; dated
between the 5th and 3rd centuries BCE, [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaogong_ji))
says: "匠人營國。方九里，旁三門。國中九經九緯，經塗九軌。左祖右社，前朝後市，市朝一夫。"
Wikisource notes that one edition has 面 instead of 前 ("前一作「面」")
([Chinese Wikisource](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/周禮/冬官考工記)). My plain reading: the
capital is nine *li* square, with three gates on each side and nine streets each way; the
ancestral temple on the left, the altar of soil and grain on the right, the court in front, the
market behind. Tang Chang'an had "more than one hundred walled wards", ward walls of "9 to 10
ft", two official markets (East and West) that opened at noon to "300 strikes on a loud drum"
and closed before dusk to "300 beats to a loud gong", and a nightly curfew lifted only for the
Lantern Festival ([Wikipedia, Chang'an](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27an)). Ward
counts disagree between sources (100, 108 or 110).

**8. Edge institutions.**

- **Cemeteries.** Rome's Twelve Tables forbade burial inside the city; an inscribed marker
  read "No burning of corpses beyond this marker in the direction of the city"
  ([Lapham's Quarterly](https://www.laphamsquarterly.org/node/4073)). Medieval Christian
  towns did the opposite: churchyards inside the walls. Paris's Holy Innocents cemetery sat
  beside the central market (Les Halles) until an edict of 4 September 1780 stopped burials
  there, because of "unsanitary conditions"
  ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Innocents%27_Cemetery)).
- **Leper houses.** Carole Rawcliffe: "almost all these houses were situated on major roads
  on the outskirts of towns and cities", sometimes "right next to gates". At least 300 were
  founded in England between the late 11th century and 1350. Other hospitals and friaries also
  sat outside "because they too needed sufficient space"
  ([Gresham College lecture 2012](https://www.gresham.ac.uk/node/12407)).
- **Gallows.** York's Tyburn was "erected on the Knavesmire" on 7 March 1379 and used until
  1801; the site lies on Tadcaster Road, a main road out of York
  ([York HER](https://her.york.gov.uk/Monument/MYO2238)). Paris's Montfaucon gibbet stood on a
  mound outside the walls near the leper house of St Lazare (blog source
  [stewross.com](https://stewross.com/gibet-de-montfaucon/), unverified, not fetched).
- **Brothels.** London in the late 14th century tolerated prostitution only in Cock Lane
  (inside the walls, by Smithfield) and in Southwark, "a district then outside the city, but
  within the jurisdiction of the Bishop of Winchester"; by the early 1400s stews were banned
  from the City ([Wellcome Collection](https://wellcomecollection.org/stories/the-bishop-s-profitable-sex-workers)).
  Rule: vice goes over the river or the boundary, to a different jurisdiction.
- **Fairs and executions.** Smithfield "once sat just outside London's medieval wall". It held
  the Friday horse and cattle market (Fitzstephen, 1174), Bartholomew Fair "for over 700 years",
  and executions such as William Wallace's in 1305
  ([London Museum](https://www.londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/london-stories/history-smithfield-market/)).
- **Faubourgs.** A faubourg is "an agglomeration forming around a throughway leading outwards
  from a city gate", and it took the name of the road inside the city (rue Saint-Denis became
  rue du Faubourg Saint-Denis) ([Wikipedia, Faubourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faubourg)).
  Who lived in faubourgs: not found in a fetched source.

**9. Markets: how many, how far apart, which one where.**

- **Spacing law (England).** Bracton (13th century): a new market is a "wrongful nuisance"
  if held "within six leagues and a half and the third part of a half" of an existing one,
  because "every reasonable day's journey consists of twenty miles" and "A day's journey is
  divided into three parts": going, trading, returning. A rival market held "on the second or
  third day or longer after the day of the other market" is not wrongful
  ([Bracton Online p.198](https://amesfoundation.law.harvard.edu/Bracton/Unframed/English/v3/198.htm),
  [p.199](https://amesfoundation.law.harvard.edu/Bracton/Unframed/English/v3/199.htm)).
  The usual reading is 6⅔ miles (a third of 20).
- **Central place theory** (Christaller, 1933) explains "the number, size and range of market
  services". *Threshold* is the minimum population that supports a good; *range* is how far
  people will travel for it. "The larger the settlements are in size, the fewer in number they
  will be" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_place_theory)). This is about
  spacing towns in a region, not markets inside one town.
- **Specialised markets.** Cattle market at the gate or just outside the wall: Smithfield
  (above). Fish market on the waterfront where boats land: Billingsgate, where "Boats delivered
  fish to this small inlet of the Thames and business was conducted on the quayside"; made a
  free fish market by an Act of 1698/1699 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billingsgate_Fish_Market)).
  Bread market on one named street: a search summary says Edward I's 1302 rule forced bakers to
  sell only in Bread Street (unverified).
- **How many markets per population:** not found.

**10. Palace, temple, market.**

- China: court in front, market behind, ancestral temple left, altar right (Kaogong ji, above).
- India: palace north of centre, gods' shrines at the centre (Arthashastra, above).
- Aztec Tenochtitlan: the walled sacred precinct with the Templo Mayor lay northeast of the
  central crossing; Moctezuma II's palace bordered the square on the east and Axayacatl's on
  the west ([Wikipedia, Zócalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z%C3%B3calo)). The great market
  was in the twin city of Tlatelolco, not beside the palace (search summary, unverified).
- Islamic city: Great Mosque at the heart, suq around it (Marçais model, a tendency, see 3).
- Medieval London: Cheapside, the main market street, ran from the Great Conduit toward St
  Paul's cathedral (Fordham, above).
- Japanese castle towns (*jōkamachi*): the castle sat high, the settlement "in a lower
  location below the mountain", with each class of building "grouped together hierarchically"
  ([JAANUS](https://projects.mcah.columbia.edu/jaanus/node/3565)). That the highest samurai
  lived between the inner and outer moats comes from travel sites (unverified).

### Case studies

1. **Amsterdam, c. 1832.** The poor at the edge. The elite in the 17th-century canal belt,
   one ring out from the busy Dam. Mixing at the scale of the street front: rich on main
   canals, poor in back courts ([TSEG 2013](https://tseg.nl/article/download/7456/8086/11198)).
2. **Medieval London.** One city shows almost every edge rule: Cheapside market street with
   trade-named side streets; slaughter banned inside the walls from 1371 with central
   exceptions; Smithfield outside the wall for cattle, fair and executions; Billingsgate on the
   quay for fish; the Hanse Steelyard as a walled riverside compound; brothels across the river
   in the Bishop of Winchester's Southwark; at least eight leprosaria on the approach roads
   (sources in Findings 2, 4, 5, 8, 9).
3. **Islamic Seville and Toledo (al-Andalus).** Hisba law moves kilns to the moat outside the
   wall. Tanners south of Toledo, away from the aqueduct. Brick makers by the ditch. Butchers
   use vats, waste carried outside ([OpenEdition](https://books.openedition.org/pur/300752),
   [Garrido Amorós](https://ucm.es/data/cont/media/www/pag-127133/Garrido%20Amor%C3%B3s,%20P..pdf)).
4. **Fez, Morocco.** The first mellah (15th century) was built beside the royal palace in the
   fortified new town Fes el-Jdid, for protection and taxation ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mellah)).
5. **Venice.** Jews locked into the Ghetto Nuovo at night from 1516. German merchants housed in
   the Fondaco dei Tedeschi at the Rialto, on the Grand Canal (sources in Findings 5).
6. **Speyer, 1084.** A bishop founds a walled Jewish settlement outside the town to attract
   trade and protect the settlers ([Fordham](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/source/1084landjews.asp)).
7. **Nagasaki, Dejima (1636-1850s).** A 120 x 75 m artificial island, one guarded bridge,
   foreign traders confined, locals barred ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dejima)).
8. **Tang Chang'an.** More than a hundred walled wards, night curfew, two walled markets with
   drum and gong hours, foreign goods in the West Market, an Ironmongers' Lane in the East
   Market ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chang%27an)).
9. **Arthashastra ideal city.** A full compass plan of castes, trades, stores, stables,
   shrines, cremation grounds and outcastes ([archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/kautilyasarthash00sham/kautilyasarthash00sham_djvu.txt)).
10. **Kaogong ji ideal capital.** Square, three gates a side, a nine-by-nine street grid, temple
    left, altar right, court front, market rear ([Wikisource](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/周禮/冬官考工記)).
11. **Damascus.** The Christian quarter around Bab Tuma in the northeast of the walled city
    ([GEDSH](https://gedsh.bethmardutho.org/Damascus)).
12. **Tenochtitlan.** The walled sacred precinct with palaces on the central square; the great
    market in the twin city Tlatelolco ([Wikipedia Zócalo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z%C3%B3calo);
    Tlatelolco detail unverified).

### What this means for a generator and its rule packs

- **Place from the outside in, using noxiousness and space as the sorting key.** Give every
  use a `noxious` level and a `space_need`. High-noxious uses (tannery, slaughter, kilns, dyers)
  default, in a pack that adopts this rule, to `on_water` + `downstream_of(drinking_water)` +
  `outside_or_edge(wall)`. The documented exceptions stay possible: long-established central
  butchers' markets (rule 7) and tanneries inside Fez (unverified); the mosque-to-edge model is
  contested. This one rule
  covers rules 6-10 in many cultures. The "downwind" variant is only sourced for industrial
  smoke, so it goes in the rule pack as a tendency, not a law.
- **Edge institutions attach to gates and roads, not just to "outside".** Leper house,
  gallows, cattle market, fair field and faubourg all need a `near_gate` or `on_road_out`
  anchor. Gallows and fairs also need open ground (a common or field). This means the
  road-and-gate stage must run before these placements.
- **Wealth needs two scales.** At district scale: poor at the edge, elite either at the
  centre (Sjoberg pack) or one ring out from a busy market (Dutch pack). At street scale:
  rich frontage on main streets, poor in back courts. The rule packs pick the district model;
  the street-front gradient applies in both.
- **Enclaves are a compound constraint type.** Jewish quarter, mellah, ghetto, fondaco,
  Steelyard and Dejima share one shape: a bounded block with few gates, an anchor (palace,
  waterfront, island, gate) and a flag for "gates locked at night". One constraint kind with
  parameters (anchor, wall yes/no, gate count) covers all of them; the anchor differs by culture.
- **Some culture packs are compass-driven.** The Arthashastra and the Kaogong ji give
  directions relative to the palace (north, east, left, right). The generator therefore needs a
  `bearing_from(anchor, direction)` constraint. European and Islamic packs do not use compass
  rules; they use distance and adjacency.
- **Markets scale with jurisdiction, not only population.** The only hard numbers found are
  Bracton's spacing between towns (about 6⅔ miles, or two days apart). Inside one town,
  specialised markets attach to their supply: fish at the quay, cattle at a gate, bread on a
  named street. I found no sourced ratio of markets to population, so the count of markets is
  a specification input or a GM? question, not a rule.
