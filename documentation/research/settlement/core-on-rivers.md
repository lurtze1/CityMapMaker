## Town cores on two or more rivers (issue #137)

Scope: real medieval towns that stand on two or more rivers or streams. Question: did the earliest
core (market place, first bridge, main church, castle) sit on the largest river, at the confluence,
on the smaller river, or elsewhere? Nothing here is about a fictional city. Every claim carries a
source number from the Sources list. Each URL was fetched during this research on 2026-11-09 unless
it says "(search snippet)": then only the search engine's summary of the page was seen, and the
claim is weak. "(Wikipedia)" marks a Wikipedia page. "(weak)" marks a tourism page or a page of
uncertain authorship. "(not in the source)" marks something I know from general knowledge that no
fetched page confirmed; it is never used in the counts.

Why the project asks: ADR-049 puts the core, when the site has no step-one road, on "the river
point nearest the centre over all rivers, a tie to the alphabetically first river". Jeffrey likes a
"biggest river" rule instead and asked for evidence (#137).

Jargon, one line each:
- **Confluence**: the point where two rivers meet; the smaller one (the tributary) ends there.
- **Tongue / promontory**: the wedge of land between two rivers just above their confluence.
- **Discharge**: the volume of water a river carries, in cubic metres per second (m³/s); the usual
  measure of "larger" for rivers.
- **Castrum / castellum / oppidum**: a Roman fort, a small Roman fort, a Celtic fortified town.
- **Kremlin / krom / detinets**: the fortified citadel of a Russian town.

### What the project already holds

- `core.world.mfm-river-order-lead`: across 62,290 half-degree cells over 2,000 years, a river one
  Strahler order bigger gave urbanisation "slightly more than 8" years earlier (cross-analysis
  core.md, row 126). This says bigger rivers attract towns; it says nothing about where inside a
  town the core sits.
- Cross-analysis core.md row 127: "A river crossing (ford, then bridge) is the seed" in Oxford,
  Wallingford, Frankfurt, Maastricht, Innsbruck, Varanasi and Dublin.
- town-plan-corpus.md §11: Luxembourg is "a plateau city on 40-60 m cliffs at a river confluence"
  (Alzette and Pétrusse) with the count's seat "at the tip of the promontory".
- timber-ports.md case 1: Viking Dublin was refounded in 917 "on a 15 m ridge along the Liffey
  above the Poddle confluence".
- city-foundation-and-growth.md: "Every fort decision in the evidence reads the terrain: hill,
  promontory, river bend, confluence, peninsula, ford"; Tobolsk (1587) at the Tobol-Irtysh
  confluence; Manching near the Paar-Danube confluence.

Three further observations:

1. **A core on a tongue touches both rivers**, so "confluence" and "largest river" are not
   exclusive. Where a source names one bank, it is the larger river's: Nantes "on the north bank of
   the Loire near its confluence with the Erdre" [21]; York "above the river Ouse, close to its
   confluence" [1]; Dublin "along the Liffey above the Poddle confluence" (timber-ports.md). Passau
   shows that "larger" is not always clear: the Inn carries more water, the Danube is longer and
   is treated as the main river [11].
2. **The first bridge often crossed the smaller river.** Lyon's first bridge crossed the Saône,
   about a century before the first Rhône bridge [7]. Koblenz's medieval stone bridge crossed the
   Moselle [9]. Bristol's name may come from the Frome bridges, not the Avon [23]. In Norwich the
   Wensum, the larger river, was the one crossed at the ford [38]. In Paris, Basel and Prague the
   first bridges crossed the largest river [29, 36, 37]. The sources do not give a reason; I do not
   know whether bridge length was the cause.
3. **Many confluences are a big river and a small stream**: Dublin (Poddle), Moscow (Neglinnaya),
   Basel (Birsig), London (Walbrook). In these the stream is a defensive ditch and a harbour pool,
   not a second river of comparable size.

### Drivers the sources name

- **Defence on a promontory between rivers**: Dublin "effectively defended by both rivers" [24];
  Namur "rocky spur" [14]; Belgrade "ending ridge" over the confluence [20]; Basel hill steep on
  three sides (search snippet) [30]; Luxembourg (town-plan-corpus.md §11).
- **Crossing point (ford or bridge)**: Frankfurt "ford of the Franks" [43]; Norwich Roman ford [38];
  Bristol "place of the bridge" [23]; Prague "práh", ford (weak) [36]; Paris island crossing [37].
- **Harbour**: Dublin's pool (timber-ports.md); Bristol's port and berthing in the Frome [22]; York's
  Roman jetties on the Foss [2].
- **Road security**: Koblenz fort to secure the Rhine valley road [9].
- No source says a core was placed on a river *because* that river was the largest.

### Limits of this sample

- Selection bias: the towns come from the issue's list and from famous confluence towns. A random
  sample of medieval towns on two rivers was not drawn; I do not know whether one exists.
- Size evidence is missing for 11 of the 21 towns; "larger" there rests on which river ends in the
  other.
- Most sources are Wikipedia. Five claims rest on search snippets only (Lyon bridge dates, Nizhny
  Novgorod, Basel's Birsig, Prague's ford, Kraków's wetlands).

### Options for the core rule (ADR-049, the no-step-one-road case)

The river rule applies only when the site has no step-one road. Truro has one river (`river`, 40 m)
and two roads that both end at `core`, so it has no step-one road and the river rule decides its
core today.

Two-river test site used below (invented for comparison, not a vault place): site 2,400 m square,
centre (1200, 1200). River `wide` (60 m) runs west to east 600 m north of the centre. River
`narrow` (15 m) runs from the south-west and joins `wide` at (1900, 1800), passing 150 m south-east
of the centre on its way.

1. **Keep the closest-to-centre rule** (ADR-049 today). Truro: the point on `river` nearest the
   centre. Test site: on `narrow`, 150 m from the centre. No source names distance to the centre as
   a driver; on this site the rule picks the smaller river, which matches 1 of 22 towns (Lyon).
2. **Largest river**: the widest river (`width_metres`), the point on it nearest the centre; a width
   tie goes to the alphabetically first river. Truro: same point as option 1. Test site: on `wide`,
   600 m north of the centre. Matches the 6 largest-river towns, and the bank of the larger river in
   13 of the 14 confluence towns, but not the confluence point itself.
3. **Confluence first, then largest river**: if two rivers meet inside the site, the core is at the
   meeting point; with several meeting points, the one nearest the centre; otherwise option 2.
   Truro: no confluence, so the same point as options 1 and 2. Test site: at (1900, 1800), about
   920 m from the centre. Matches 14 of 22 towns directly and falls back to the 6 largest-river
   towns.
4. **Confluence first, then closest to centre**: as option 3, falling back to option 1. Truro: same
   point. Test site: at the confluence, as option 3.

On Truro all four options give the same core, because Truro has one river. They differ only on
sites with two or more rivers.

Open point the research does not settle: I do not know whether the specification can express a
confluence. In `specifications/example.yaml` and `specifications/truro.yaml` every river runs from
one compass region to another, so two rivers can cross but neither ends in the other. Options 3
and 4 need either a river that ends at another river, or treating a crossing of two rivers as a
confluence.

**Question for Jeffrey:** Which rule should place the core when the site has no step-one road?
1. Keep the closest-to-centre rule (ADR-049 as it is).
2. Largest river: the widest river, the point on it nearest the centre.
3. Confluence first, then the largest river.
4. Confluence first, then the closest-to-centre rule.
