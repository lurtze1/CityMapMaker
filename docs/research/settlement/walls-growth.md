## Walls and growth over time

Research date: 2026-10-20. Every number below comes from a page listed under Sources that
was actually fetched in this session. Where two fetched sources disagree, both numbers are
given. Arithmetic I did myself is marked "(my calculation)". Things I could not verify are
marked "(unverified)".

Note on the requested books: Creighton & Higham *Medieval Town Walls*, Tracy (ed.) *City
Walls*, Kostof *The City Assembled* and Le Strange were **not** available online in a form
I could fetch. Nothing below is taken from them. See "Not found".

### Findings

1. **Walls run 1 to 14 km and enclose 10 to 1,150 ha.** Small Welsh planted towns sit at
   the bottom (Conwy: 1.3 km, 10 ha). Big medieval cities sit around 8 km and 400 to 650 ha
   (Cologne, Florence). Chinese imperial capitals are larger again (Xi'an Ming wall:
   13.74 km, 11.52 km²).
2. **Gate counts are small and fairly stable.** Fetched examples range from 2 gatehouses
   (Caernarfon, 734 m) to 15 (Paris c. 1200, Kano). Big circuits do not get proportionally
   more gates: Cologne's 8 km wall had 12 gates; Xi'an's 13.7 km wall has 4 main gates.
   One gate per 0.5 to 1 km of wall is typical in the table (my calculation).
3. **Tower spacing follows weapon range.** Vitruvius: towers "not more than a bowshot
   apart". Measured: Ávila about 28.6 m (my calculation, 2,516 m / 88 towers), Conwy about
   46 m, Paris (Philip Augustus) 60 m, Xi'an ramparts 120 m. The Xi'an source says the
   spacing was chosen so the gap was "within the range of an arrow shot from either side".
4. **Ditches are roughly 10 to 20 m wide.** York medieval ditch about 18.3 m wide and 3 m
   deep; Prague Old Town moat 10 m wide and 8 m deep; Xi'an moat 18 m wide and 6 m deep.
   Gunpowder-era works add a glacis (a bare earth slope outside the ditch, so attackers
   have no cover). Vienna's glacis was about 500 m wide, with building banned.
5. **There was often a lane just inside the wall.** Newcastle had one "presumably
   originally right round the circuit"; a 1330 record orders space kept so the mayor could
   "ride for custody and defence". Norwich had "the way inside the wall" and a lane outside
   the ditch too. The Roman *pomerium* (a sacred boundary strip) covered ground on both
   sides of Rome's wall; building and burial there were banned in principle. No width was
   found for any of these.
6. **Walls were not tight skins.** In Hausa cities (Kano and others) walls "normally
   enclosed an area two or three times as large as the town itself" so farmland could be
   worked in a siege. Siena in the 1200s had "vineyards and vegetable gardens" inside the
   walls while in other parts "building had outgrown walls". Bristol c. 1300: 55 ha walled
   but 130 ha settled once suburbs are counted.
7. **Cities grew in rings.** Cologne: Roman 25 ha (kuladig; see conflict below), 950s
   about 122 ha, 1141 about 223 ha, 1259 401 ha with 12 gates. Paris: 253 ha (1190-1213)
   then 440 ha (1356-1383). Nördlingen's 1327 wall made the walled area four times bigger.
   Prague's New Town (1348) was about 250 ha, beside the 106 ha Old Town.
8. **Dead walls became ring roads, boulevards and parks.** Vienna Ringstraße (decree 1857,
   5.3 km, palaces, museums, parks on the glacis). Moscow Garden Ring (rampart levelled
   1818-1826, 16 km, 22 to 43 m wide at first) and Boulevard Ring (1820s). Paris Grands
   Boulevards (after 1670). Florence viali (Poggi, after 1865). Cologne Ringe (from 1881).
   Prague Na Příkopě literally means "on the moat" (moat covered 1760). Winchester and
   Newcastle keep street names "North Walls", "West Walls".
9. **Fringe belts** (Conzen): belts of big-plot, loosely built land (institutions,
   cemeteries, parks, barracks, industry, villas) form at the town edge when outward growth
   stalls, then get swallowed when growth resumes. In walled towns the wall is the
   "fixation line" (a barrier that holds the edge in one place for a long time) and the
   inner fringe belt forms around it. Whitehand explains why: house-builders outbid
   institutions for edge land in booms; in slumps institutions get the land.
10. **Suburbs grow along roads out of gates.** Siena's built-up area "sprawled along two
    principal roads". Winchester's Hyde Street suburb runs north from the (demolished)
    North Gate, across the old city ditch. Totnes had suburbs outside its West Gate by the
    12th-13th century. People living just outside the walls were often poor (Perugia 1285,
    per Waley).

### Growth patterns

**1. Successive wall rings (concentric growth).** The town fills its wall, suburbs grow
outside the gates, then a new wall encloses the suburbs. The old wall is left standing,
built over, or levelled.
- Cologne: 25 ha (Roman) -> about 122 ha (950s) -> about 223 ha (by 1141) -> 401 ha (1180-1259). [Kuladig]
- Paris: 253 ha (Philip Augustus, c. 1213) -> 440 ha (Charles V, 1383, right bank only; the old right-bank wall was left "disused but not demolished"). [W-PhilipII], [W-CharlesV]
- Florence: six circuits are counted on one site (Roman, Carolingian 870, Matildine 1078, first communal 1172-1175, second communal 1284-1333, 16th-century bastions); "some scholars count only four". [CT-Florence]
- Moscow: Bely Gorod wall -> Boulevard Ring; Zemlyanoy Val rampart -> Garden Ring. [W-Boulevard], [W-GardenRing]
- Plan trace: concentric or nested street rings; old gate sites become squares or
  crossroads on the ring; radial roads run straight through all rings.

**2. Ribbon suburbs outside gates.** Houses line the main roads just outside each gate,
often across the ditch on a bridge.
- Winchester: Hyde Street suburb outside North Gate, with a buried bridge "over the former City ditch outside North Gate". [Winchester]
- Totnes: medieval suburbs outside the West Gate, 12th-13th c. (search snippet only, unverified).
- Siena: built-up area "sprawled along two principal roads". [Waley]
- Plan trace: long, narrow fingers of plots along radial roads, with fields between them.

**3. Planned extension beside the old town.** A ruler or council lays out a new,
regular quarter next to the old, irregular one.
- Prague New Town (1348, Charles IV): about 250 ha next to the 106 ha Old Town, its own 3.5 km wall, 4 gates, three huge markets (cattle market about 550 x 150 m). [W-PragueNT]
- Edinburgh New Town (competition 1766, James Craig): axial grid on a ridge, a main street between two garden squares, mews lanes behind; reached by draining the Nor Loch and building North Bridge (1772) and the Mound. Old Town overcrowding had reached "breaking point". [W-EdinNT]
- Plan trace: a sharp seam between an irregular old core and a grid; the seam is often a
  filled moat or drained loch that becomes a wide street (Prague Na Příkopě/Národní,
  Edinburgh Princes Street Gardens).

**4. Infill / repletion inside the wall.** Back plots (the long rear parts of house
plots) get built over.
- Conzen calls the inside-wall zone "associated with repletion inside the wall through secondary building development on tail-end plots of the already built-up area". [Unlu]
- Plan trace: courts and alleys entering long narrow plots from the street.

**5. Demolished wall becomes ring road / boulevard.**
- Vienna: decree 1857, 5.3 km Ring, palaces, museums, opera, parliament, university, parks on the former glacis; a parallel cargo road on the glacis' outer edge. [W-Ring]
- Florence: Poggi's "Viali di Circonvallazione" after 1865; gates and Oltrarno walls kept. [FUA], [CT-Florence]
- Paris: Louis XIII wall destroyed 1670, later the Grands Boulevards. [W-CharlesV]
- Moscow Garden Ring: 22-43 m wide when made. [W-GardenRing]
- Prague: moat (10 m wide) covered 1760, planted with chestnut trees, became Na Příkopě. [W-Prikope]
- Plan trace: a wide, continuous ring street exactly where the wall plus ditch was, with
  big public buildings and parks along it, and the old gate positions as squares.

**6. Growth along water.** Not researched in enough depth to give examples with sources.
See "Not found".

### Fringe belts

- **Definition** (Conzen 1969, quoted in Ünlü 2022): "a belt-like zone originating from the
  temporarily stationary or slowly advancing fringe of a town and composed of a
  characteristic mixture of land use units initially seeking peripheral location". [Unlu]
- **Typical uses** (same source): industry (warehouses, factories, transport, quarries);
  institutions (military barracks, government, religious, health, education); open space
  (public parks, cemeteries, nurseries); recreation (sports fields, golf courses); villas
  and isolated large houses. Hiatus-period uses also listed as hospitals, universities,
  jails (search snippet; consistent with the fetched paper).
- **Look on a map:** plots vary a lot in size, buildings cover little of each plot, often
  no buildings face the street, "coarse-grained" compared with housing. [Unlu]
- **Three belts at Alnwick:** inner, middle, outer. The inner belt sits around the town
  wall (the fixation line) and is the most continuous; the outer is the most broken. The
  wall splits the inner belt in two: inside (infill on rear plots, tight grain) and
  outside (more open, sometimes scattered). Conzen describes the inner belt "with its
  fixation line and consequent ring road". [Unlu]
- **Origin:** Herbert Louis (1936) saw three rings around Berlin: the inner one with
  museums (Museum Island), the cathedral and open space; the second around the early
  suburbs with the Tiergarten; the third scattered. [Unlu]
- **Why they form (Whitehand):** house-builders pay high rents near the edge, little
  farther out; institutions care less about distance. So in housing booms the edge is
  built with houses; in slumps institutions take the edge land. Result: alternating rings
  of housing and institutions. [Unlu]
- **No wall, no clear belt:** towns without a wall or natural barrier "would be unlikely
  to develop continuous fringe belts"; fringe belts are less visible in American cities.
  [Unlu]
- **Planned fringe belts:** Vienna's Ringstraße and Copenhagen's public spaces on its
  demolished walls are cited as planned fringe-belt zones. [Unlu]
- **Later fate:** belts can survive (schools, hospitals stay), be replaced by another
  fringe use, expand, or be "alienated" (turned into housing or shops). [Unlu]
- **Other culture:** Istanbul: the Edirnekapı cemetery outside the Theodosian land wall is
  treated as a fringe-belt unit; market gardens near the wall have been rezoned for
  housing. [Hazar] (that PDF's text extraction was partly garbled; details thin.)
- **Widths:** not found. No fetched source gives a typical fringe belt width in metres.

### What this means for a generator

- **Wall from area, not area from wall.** Pick the enclosed area from the town size
  (10 ha planted town to about 400-650 ha great city), then draw a circuit. Real circuits
  in the table run about 1.3 km for 10 ha up to 8.5 km for 430-650 ha. Let the wall
  enclose some empty land (gardens, vineyards, convent grounds); for a Hausa-style rule
  pack, 2 to 3 times the built area.
- **Gates: 2 to 15, roughly one per main road, not per km.** Larger circuits get fewer
  gates per km. Put a tower every 30 to 60 m on a medieval curtain (bowshot), about 120 m
  on a Chinese-style rampart wall. Ditch 10 to 20 m wide. Keep an optional lane just
  inside the wall (intramural lane); its width was not found, so the rule pack decides it.
- **Growth as layered rings.** Model each era as: fill the wall (rear-plot infill) ->
  ribbon suburbs along radial roads outside gates -> fringe-belt uses (cemetery, hospital,
  convent, barracks, market ground) in the ring just outside the wall -> optional new
  wall around everything. Cologne's 25 -> 122 -> 223 -> 401 ha sequence is a good
  calibration curve: each new ring is about 1.8 to 4.9 times the area of the previous
  one (my calculation).
- **Old walls leave traces.** When a ring is retired, replace wall + ditch with a wide
  ring street (Moscow Garden Ring 22-43 m), keep some gates as squares or monuments, and
  place large public buildings and parks on the freed strip (Vienna). Street names like
  "North Walls" or "On the Moat" are a cheap lore hook.
- **Planned extension as an alternative.** Offer a rule that adds a regular grid quarter
  next to the old core (Prague New Town, Edinburgh New Town), joined across a filled moat
  or valley by a few bridges or a single wide street.
- **Fringe belts need a slow period.** A generator that tracks growth phases should put
  big-plot institutional uses at the edge during "slow" phases and dense housing during
  "fast" phases; this gives the alternating rings Whitehand describes.
