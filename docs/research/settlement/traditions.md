# Research: town-building rules by culture

Date: 2026-10-20. Scope: rules that decided where things went in pre-1900 towns outside Europe,
plus a short European and Spanish-colonial comparison. Each rule is labelled with how strong it was:

- **law**: written and enforced by a court, a ruler or a judge.
- **treatise ideal**: written in a book of how a town *should* be; real towns may ignore it.
- **observed practice**: what scholars or travellers see in real towns, with no written rule found.

Every rule has a source I fetched. "(unverified)" means I saw it only in a search-result
summary, not on a page I opened. Most sources are Wikipedia or secondary scholarly summaries;
primary texts in translation are marked as such. Treat Wikipedia-only claims as a starting point.

## Town-building rules by culture

### Islamic cities (Arab world, North Africa, Ottoman lands)

Jargon: *fina* = the strip of space in front of a house wall that the owner controls but does
not own outright. *darar* = harm. *waqf* = a charitable endowment; property given to it can
never be sold. *hisba* = the office of the market inspector (*muhtasib*); hisba manuals are his
rulebooks. *Maliki* = one of the four Sunni law schools, dominant in North Africa and Spain.

Rules:
- When neighbours dispute a path, it is made seven cubits wide. **law** (a saying of the
  Prophet, Sahih Muslim 1613, primary text in translation). A cubit was 49.5 to 80 cm
  depending on place and period, so this is roughly 3.5 to 5.5 m. **observed practice**
  (al-Hathloul). Real street widths often differed from seven cubits (unverified; search
  summary only).
- Through streets are public, everyone has right of way. Lanes and dead ends are
  semi-private, owned in common by the houses on them. **law** (Maliki jurists, per
  al-Hathloul).
- On a through street, a house's fina reaches at most half the street width. On a dead-end
  lane it covers the whole lane width. **law** (attributed to Caliph Umar, adopted by Maliki
  jurists). Hakim gives the fina as about 1.0 to 1.5 m alongside walls that face a street.
  **treatise / scholarly reconstruction**.
- The fina may be used (benches, planters, balconies) only if passage is not narrowed and no
  harm results. The owner must keep it clear of mud, rainwater and rubbish. **law**.
- The harm rule: "no harm and no reciprocal harm". Smoke, smell, noise and vibration count
  as harm. **law** (hadith "la darar wa la dirar", applied by judges).
- Polluting trades (furnaces, tanneries) go outside the walls. Bake ovens may stay inside.
  **law / observed practice** (al-Hathloul).
- A projection over a street must clear a rider on the largest camel saddle. **law**
  (Ibn al-Rami, Tunis, 14th c.).
- A structure may never roof a whole street and cut off light and air. **law**.
- A new window that overlooks a neighbour must be sealed; an old one may stay but may not be
  used to look in. In Medina (1573) a court raised windows to about 220 cm above the floor.
  **law** (court cases).
- Doors of facing houses must not face each other directly. **law** (Maliki). In practice,
  only two facing pairs were found in 200+ houses in one Medina quarter. **observed practice**.
- First built wins: whoever builds first keeps his projection or window; later neighbours
  adapt. **law** (Ibn Sha'ban; the "old vs new opening" rule).
- Gates across residential lanes were customary (Tunis) and removed only if they caused
  harm. **observed practice**.
- A neighbour may not stop a neighbour from resting beams in his wall (party walls are
  shared). **law** (saying attributed to the Prophet, cited by Hakim).
- Market inspector: no sitting in narrow passages, no roofing alleys, no gutters draining
  into narrow alleys. **law** (Ibn al-Ukhuwwa, hisba manual, d. 1329).
- Markets: high-status trades (fine cloth, jewellery, books, spices) sit in the souks around
  the Friday mosque; big open markets for livestock and food sit outside the gates.
  **observed practice** (Kennedy 1985 on Syria, quoted by Williams).
- Waqf: endowed property cannot be sold, so mosques, baths, fountains, inns and the shops
  that fund them stay fixed for centuries. Most of greater Zanzibar Stone Town became waqf.
  **law**.
- There were no numeric zoning rules. Experts were called in case by case. **observed
  practice** (al-Hathloul: "no quantitative rules existed").

Example towns: Medina, Tunis, Riyadh (old), Fez, Damascus, Baghdad (al-Karkh markets).

How far practice followed: These are *process* rules, enforced only when a neighbour
complained. They make the town grow cell by cell; they do not draw a plan. The "Islamic city"
as one fixed model (mosque in the middle, souks around, then houses) is disputed:
Janet Abu-Lughod (1987) called it an Orientalist construct built on a few cases (Fez, Syria)
and doubted that Islamic law alone explains the cellular structure, which predates Islam.

Sources:

### China

Jargon: *Kaogong ji* = "Record of Trades", a chapter of the *Rites of Zhou* (c. 3rd c. BCE)
that includes an ideal capital. *li* = a length unit, about 400–500 m. *fang* = a walled
ward. *yamen* = government office.

Rules:
- The capital is a square, nine li a side, with three gates per side. Nine north-south and
  nine east-west streets; north-south streets are nine carriage tracks wide.
  **treatise ideal** (Kaogong ji, Wenren translation, quoted on Wikipedia).
- Palace at the centre; ancestral temple on the left (east), altar of earth on the right
  (west), market behind (north). **treatise ideal** (CGTN summary of the Kaogong ji). Left
  and right are as seen by the ruler facing south.
- The principal gate is in the middle of the south wall; gates are symmetric; wall size
  follows the city's rank in the administrative hierarchy. **observed practice** (Chinese
  city wall article).
- Tang Chang'an: 12 gates (three per side); walled wards of about 1 × 1 li; wide avenues
  (25 m narrowest, 100 m to the gates, 150 m Imperial Way) that also act as firebreaks;
  palace in the north, not the centre; trade only in two official markets (East and West),
  opened at noon by 300 drum beats and closed before dusk by 300 gong beats (the curfew).
  **law** (Tang regulations, per Wikipedia).
- Song collapse: the curfew was abolished (1063 per Wikipedia), markets ran at all hours, and
  houses and shops were built along streets instead of inside walled wards. **observed
  practice** (dating disputed among scholars: unverified).
- County seats: under the first Ming emperor, City God temples and county offices were built
  across China to the same specifications (unverified; search summary only). In Pingyao,
  streets are at right angles, parallel to four main streets that run from the centre to the
  cardinal points; the south street has temples and civil offices east, monasteries and
  military offices west; six gates. **observed practice**.
- Feng shui site model: mountains behind (north), water in front, guardian hills left (Blue
  Dragon) and right (White Tiger), a central "energy spot". **treatise / practitioner
  tradition** (source is a feng shui teacher, not a scholar). One practitioner source calls
  "mountain behind, water in front" an oversimplification (unverified).
- Beijing (Yuan onwards) follows the Kaogong ji through a north-south central axis.
  **observed practice** (CGTN).

Example towns: Chang'an (Tang), Kaifeng (Song), Beijing, Pingyao.

How far practice followed: The Kaogong ji was an ideal that later capitals invoked selectively
(axis, square, gates in threes). Chang'an broke it by putting the palace in the north. The
ward system was real law in the Tang and faded in the Song.

### Japan

Jargon: *ritsuryo* = the 7th–10th c. legal state copied from Tang China. *jo / bo / cho* =
row, column and block of the capital grid. *jokamachi* = castle town (c. 1580–1870).
*teramachi* = temple district.

Rules (grid capitals):
- Fujiwara-kyo (694): palace near the centre, following the Kaogong ji rather than Chang'an.
  **treatise ideal, applied**.
- Heijo-kyo (710) and Heian-kyo (794): palace in the north centre; a wide central avenue
  (Suzaku-oji, about 74 m at Heijo-kyo) runs south to the Rajomon gate; it splits the city
  into Left (east) and Right (west) capitals. **law** (state plan).
- Heian-kyo grid: nine jo east-west, four bo per side, 16 cho per bo, each cho about 121 m
  square. East Market in the Left capital, West Market in the Right, both in the 7th jo. Two
  state temples near the south gate. No continuous city wall. **law** (state plan).

Rules (castle towns):
- Higher-ranked samurai live closer to the castle. **observed practice** ("in principle").
- Foot soldiers (ashigaru) live at the outer edge of the merchant districts. **observed practice**.
- Merchants and craftsmen live outside the samurai districts, grouped by trade; lots are
  narrow-fronted and deep ("eel's nests"). **observed practice** (ward names like Daiku-machi,
  Kaji-machi = carpenters', smiths' town).
- Temples are placed on the outer rim as a defensive belt. **observed practice**. Hideyoshi
  moved many temples to Teramachi in Kyoto in 1590, said to slow attackers from the east.
- Main road passes the front of the castle; roads crank or dead-end to lengthen the route to
  the castle; houses line the main street to block the view of the castle. **observed practice**.
- Sections had wooden gates shut at night. **observed practice**.

Example towns: Heian-kyo (Kyoto), Heijo-kyo (Nara), castle towns generally.

How far practice followed: The grid capitals were never fully built. Heian-kyo's western half
declined and the city spread north and east. Castle-town zoning by class was close to universal
but "in principle", not measured.

Sources:
- Heian-kyo: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heian-ky%C5%8D
- Heijo-kyo: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heij%C5%8D-ky%C5%8D
- Fujiwara-kyo: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fujiwara-ky%C5%8D
- Jokamachi: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C5%8Dkamachi
- Teramachi Street: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teramachi_Street

### India

Jargon: *Arthashastra* = a Sanskrit statecraft manual (Kautilya, roughly 3rd c. BCE–3rd c. CE).
*Manasara* = a Sanskrit architecture treatise (Wikipedia dates it c. 700 CE). *vastu shastra* =
the tradition of such treatises. *danda* = a length unit of 6 feet (Shamasastry). *varna* =
the four classes: Brahmin (priest), Kshatriya (warrior), Vaishya (merchant), Shudra (labourer).

Rules (Arthashastra, Book II ch. 4, Shamasastry translation 1915, primary text):
- Three royal roads east-west and three north-south. **treatise ideal**.
- Road widths: main roads 4 danda (24 ft); roads to military stations, cremation grounds and
  villages 8 danda; chariot roads 5 aratni (7.5 ft); roads for men and small animals 2 aratni.
  **treatise ideal**.
- Twelve gates. **treatise ideal**.
- Palace north of the centre, facing north or east, taking one ninth of the site.
  **treatise ideal**.
- Quarter by direction: north = Brahmins, city deity, jewel and iron workers; east = Kshatriyas,
  merchants in scents, grain, liquids, artisans; south = Vaishyas, superintendents, traders in
  cooked food, liquor, meat, prostitutes, musicians; west = Shudras, crafts in thread, bamboo,
  leather, armour. Shops and hospitals north by west. **treatise ideal**.
- Corners hold guilds and corporations. Centre holds shrines to several gods. **treatise ideal**.
- Cremation grounds north or east of the city (highest class: south). Heretics and Chandalas
  (outcastes) live beyond the cremation grounds. Breaking this is fined. **treatise ideal,
  phrased as law**.
- One well per ten houses. **treatise ideal**.

Rules (Manasara plan types; source is an Indian college lecture deck, secondary):
- Dandaka: long, length twice breadth, straight streets crossing at right angles; temples at
  the edges. Sarvatobhadra: square, one to five main streets plus a ring street, temples or
  rest houses at the four corners, gates at the four directions, trades zoned by plot, centre
  left empty. Padmaka (lotus): four to eight streets, no street across the middle, gates at
  four directions, radial. Swastika: square, 81 divisions, cross streets with bent arms.
  Prastara: 16 square blocks, three streets each way. Nandyavarta: centre open or a shrine.
  Karmuka: bow-shaped, for river or sea fronts. Chaturmukha: ring street with four gates.
  **treatise ideal**. All but Padmaka and Swastika are twice as long as wide.
- A temple or assembly hall at the town centre; site slopes east, near water. **treatise
  ideal** (Wikipedia, Hindu architecture).
- The Manasara's 32 ground plans are square grids of plots (16, 25, 64, 81 squares).
  **treatise ideal** (wisdomlib).

Rules (temple towns):
- Madurai: concentric square streets around the temple, named after Tamil months and their
  festivals; temple chariots grow larger on the outer streets; wealthy and high-status
  residents nearer the temple, poorest at the edge. Redesigned by Viswanatha Nayak (1529–64)
  on shilpa shastra principles. **observed practice following a treatise**.

Example towns: Madurai; Jaipur (linked to Prastara by scholars, unverified); Sadasivapet
(Sarvatobhadra, unverified).

How far practice followed: The Arthashastra is a prescriptive ideal; I found no evidence of a
town built exactly to it. The Manasara types are a menu, not law. Temple towns like Madurai do
show the concentric, status-graded pattern.

Sources:
- Arthashastra II.4, Shamasastry: https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/kautilya-arthashastra/d/doc365604.html
- Manasara plan types (lecture deck, F. Rahman): https://www.lingayasvidyapeeth.edu.in/sanmax/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Ancient-India-Town-Planning.pdf
- Manasara 32 plans: https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/manasara-english-translation/d/doc421054.html
- Hindu architecture: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_architecture
- Madurai: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madurai

### Southeast Asia (Khmer, Burmese)

Jargon: *mandala city* = a capital laid out as a model of the cosmos, with Mount Meru (the
mountain at the centre of the universe) at its centre.

Rules:
- Angkor Thom (late 12th c.): square, 3 km a side, 8 m walls, moat; gates at the four cardinal
  points plus a fifth (Victory Gate); roads from the gates lead to the Bayon temple at the
  exact centre; corner shrines; causeways over the moat. Archaeologists read the walls and moat
  as the mountains and ocean around Mount Meru. **observed practice with cosmological intent**.
- Mandalay (1857): square citadel, walls about 2 km a side, three gates per side (12),
  each tied to a zodiac sign, wide moat; grid of 144 blocks with a 16-block palace at the centre;
  palace faces east. **law** (royal plan). The palace as Mount Meru is stated only in search
  summaries (unverified), and Tainturier (2021) argues Mandalay's founders saw it as a sanctuary
  rather than a cosmic city (unverified; review page blocked).

Example towns: Angkor Thom, Mandalay.

How far practice followed: Royal capitals followed the square-and-centre pattern closely
because the king built them at once. I found nothing on ordinary Khmer or Thai market towns.

Sources:
- Angkor Thom: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angkor_Thom
- Mandalay Palace: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandalay_Palace
- Mandalay: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandalay

### Mesoamerica and the Andes

Jargon: *calpolli* = a kin-based ward of commoners holding land in common. *hanan / hurin* =
upper and lower halves in Andean society. *kancha* = an Inca walled compound.

Rules:
- Teotihuacan: one grid orientation adopted citywide, skewed about 15.5° and 16.5° clockwise
  from north, part of a Mesoamerican "17° family" of orientations; Sprajc argues the angles
  were set by sunrise and sunset on calendar dates. A river was diverted to fit the grid.
  **observed practice** (archaeological). Avenue of the Dead about 40 m wide. Higher-status
  homes near the centre and along the avenue; no clear zoning districts.
- Tenochtitlan: four quarters, each with about 20 calpolli; a walled ceremonial precinct of about
  500 m a side at the centre; causeways to the mainland (north, south, west); canals; each
  calpolli had its own market; the main market was in Tlatelolco. Calpolli also ran schools
  for young men. **observed practice**.
- Cusco: two halves (hanan and hurin), each with two of the four quarters; a road from each
  quarter to the matching quarter of the empire; palaces around the main square, one per ruler.
  Puma-shaped plan is a popular belief. **observed practice**.
- Inca blocks: the kancha is a rectangular enclosure of three or more buildings around a
  courtyard. Ollantaytambo: four streets one way, seven the other, two kanchas per block, a
  plaza open to the east. **observed practice**.

Example towns: Teotihuacan, Tenochtitlan, Cusco, Ollantaytambo.

How far practice followed: No written rules survive (unverified as an absolute; none found).
All rules here are read from the ground by archaeologists.

### Sub-Saharan Africa

Jargon: *afin* = Yoruba palace. *oja oba* = the king's market. *birni* = Hausa walled town.
*Sarkin Kofa* = gatekeeper. *mitaa* = Swahili town district. *ikhanda* = Zulu royal or
military homestead; *kraal* = a homestead around a cattle pen.

Rules:
- Yoruba: the oba's palace is at the sacred centre; the king's market (Oja-Oba) next to it is the
  central business district; open spaces form a hierarchy; green wedges (oko etile) hold farms
  inside the town. Plans may be radial, elliptical, circular or grid. Ranked compounds: palace
  largest, then ward or lineage chiefs, then family elders. Mud walls around most towns.
  **observed practice**.
- Hausa: the walled city holds the emir's palace, Friday mosque and market at its centre
  (Zaria, unverified; search summary only); 43 administrative wards in Zaria (unverified).
  Kano's wall was over eleven miles long with thirteen gates, 30–50 ft high, fronted by a
  thorn-filled ditch, gatehouses in re-entrant angles (Foyle, c. 1959); Wikipedia gives 15 gates.
  Each gate had a named hereditary keeper; gates shut at night and in war. Kano's Kurmi market is
  laid out in lanes, one trade per lane (unverified). **observed practice**.
- Swahili: stone houses for the elite in the core (Lamu's Mkomani), mud-and-thatch in the outer
  areas (Lamu's Langoni); each district (mitaa) is organised around a mosque; in Zanzibar main
  streets run off in four directions from the central mosque; courtyard houses with a blank wall
  blocking the view inside; Lamu faces north towards Mecca; Gedi's inner wall was a social, not
  military, barrier. **observed practice**.
- Zulu homestead: a ring of huts around a central cattle pen, outer fence; the chief's (or his
  mother's) hut opposite the entrance; wives' huts next to his; sons left, daughters right,
  guests nearest the entrance; built on a slope with the entrance downhill. **observed practice**.
  Royal amakhanda: not found beyond this homestead pattern.

Example towns: Oyo, Ile-Ife, Abeokuta; Kano, Zaria, Katsina; Lamu, Zanzibar, Gedi.

How far practice followed: No codified layout law was found for any of these. They are strong
customs, visible across many towns.

### Contrast: medieval European bastides and Spanish Laws of the Indies (layout only)

Jargon: *bastide* = a planned new town in southern France, 13th–14th c. *paréage* = a contract
between a landholder and a ruler to found one. *couverts* = arcades around the market square.

Rules:
- Bastides: grid of streets, blocks often crossed by a narrow lane; a central market square with
  arcades and a covered weighing hall, usually off the main traffic axis; church not on the square
  but at an angle to it; uniform house lots, commonly 8 × 24 m; streets 6–10 m, alleys 5–6 m; each
  settler gets a house lot, a garden lot and a field. **law** (founding charter) for rights and
  lots; layout is **observed practice**.
- Laws of the Indies (1573): the main plaza is the starting point of the town, at the centre
  inland and at the shore on the coast; length at least 1.5 × width; size scaled to population,
  from 200 × 300 ft to 530 × 800 ft, with 400 × 600 ft "a good proportion"; streets laid out by
  prevailing winds; church on its own block separated from the plaza by a street, raised; town
  hall flanking the plaza, arcades on the other blocks; wide streets in cold places, narrow in
  hot; hospital for contagious disease away from the church; slaughterhouses and tanneries where
  waste can be removed; common land around the town. **law**.

How far practice followed: Bastides varied but share the square-and-grid core. Spanish colonial
towns kept the plaza-church-hall core everywhere (Mexico City, Lima) but plaza sizes varied and
coastal towns often sat on islets.

Sources:
- Bastide: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bastide
- Laws of the Indies: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laws_of_the_Indies
- Ordinance 112 and plaza sizes quoted: https://homesteadmuseum.blog/2016/11/14/at-our-leisure-the-plaza-the-first-park-in-los-angeles/

### What this means for rule packs

- **Two rule kinds, not one.** Some traditions are *plans* (Kaogong ji, Arthashastra, Manasara,
  Laws of the Indies, Mandalay): fixed slots placed top-down at founding. Others are *processes*
  (Islamic neighbour law, Song street commerce, Yoruba and Swahili custom): local constraints
  checked as each building is added. A pack needs both a "template" part (gates per side, palace
  slot, centre use) and a "local constraint" part (min street width, no facing doors, fina depth,
  noxious trade outside wall).
- **Directional zoning is a common primitive.** Arthashastra (class by compass point), Kaogong ji
  (temple left, altar right, market behind), Pingyao (civil east, military west), Zulu kraal
  (sons left, daughters right) all assign uses by direction relative to a centre and a facing.
  One constraint kind, "district X lies in sector Y relative to anchor Z facing F", covers them.
- **Distance-from-centre gradients are the other common primitive.** Status by ring appears in
  castle towns (rank to castle), Madurai (status to temple), Yoruba (chiefs near palace), Islamic
  souks (high-status trades near mosque, livestock outside gates), Swahili (stone core, mud edge).
  Encode as "rank decreases with distance from anchor" with the anchor named in the pack.
- **Numbers to put in packs, with units recorded per pack:** seven cubits (with cubit length as a
  parameter, 49.5–80 cm), fina 1.0–1.5 m, Arthashastra road widths in danda and aratni, Heian cho
  about 121 m, bastide lots 8 × 24 m and streets 6–10 m, Indies plaza 200×300 to 530×800 ft,
  Teotihuacan grid skew 15.5° / 16.5°.
- **Strength labels belong in the data.** Each rule should carry its strength (law / treatise
  ideal / observed practice) so the generator can treat law and treatise rules as hard and
  observed practice as soft, and so a GM can loosen an "ideal" capital into a lived-in town.
- **Grid orientation is a pack choice, not always north.** Cardinal (Chinese, Japanese, Indian,
  Khmer, Burmese), astronomical skew (Teotihuacan), wind-driven (Laws of the Indies) or toward
  Mecca (Lamu houses). The core needs an orientation parameter per pack.
