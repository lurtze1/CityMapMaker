## Parcels, buildings and density

> **Corrections after verification (2026-10-20):** see `verification-population.md`. The Cesaretti formula is verified and the unit is hectares (27 / 85 / 270 ha for 2,000 / 10,000 / 50,000 people). "Russell 100-120 per ha" is a figure about ancient cities, not medieval ones; use Cesaretti's own data instead (median 114 per ha, middle half 93-152; 11% of towns above 200). The Roman 150-250 per ha band has no traceable source; use Hanson, Ortman and Lobo 2017 (people = 41.834 × area^1.336, so density rises with size). Classic Maya cores are about 5 per ha (Tikal, Caracol), not 10-20; 12-28 applies to compact northern cities only. Tenochtitlan 212,500 on 1,350 ha is verified.

Research date: 2026-10-20. Every number below has a URL that was fetched during this research,
unless it is marked "(unverified)". "(unverified)" means the number came only from a search-engine
summary, or the page could not be opened (403/410 error). Arithmetic that is mine, not the source's,
is marked "my arithmetic".

Jargon used below:
- **Density**: people per hectare (1 ha = 100 m x 100 m = 2.47 acres).
- **Burgage plot**: a long, narrow rented plot in a medieval English or Scottish town, with its
  short side on the street.
- **Frontage**: the width of a plot along the street.
- **Backland**: the rear part of a plot, behind the street-front building.
- **Perch** (also rod or pole): an English length unit, 16.5 feet = about 5.03 m.
- **Household multiplier**: the number of people historians assume per recorded household or
  hearth, used to turn a tax list into a population.
- **Scaling law**: a formula of the form `area = a * population^b`. If `b` is below 1, bigger
  towns are denser.

### Findings

**1. How historians estimate town population.**
Medieval writers recorded "hearths, soldiers, taxpayers and citizens", and historians multiply
those counts up to a total population (Cesaretti et al. 2016, [PMC5051806]). The multiplier is the
weak point. For Domesday England (1086) it ranges from 3.5 to 5.0 people per household. Maitland
chose 5; J.C. Russell argued for 3.5; later work on tax records points to 4.5 to 5.0 (Bailey,
*Records of Buckinghamshire* 42, [ADS-Bailey]). Urban households were smaller than rural ones:
Florence c.1430 had 3.8 people per hearth in the city and 4.4 in the countryside; Prato had 3.7 in
the city and 5 in the countryside ([Medievalists-family]). Paris in 1328 was estimated from a
count of 61,098 hearths ([Baghdad-London]). Where no counts exist, historians use physical
proxies. The "From Baghdad to London" data set assumed roughly 1,000 inhabitants per mosque or
public bath (hammam) for Islamic cities ([Baghdad-London]). For Roman Pompeii, J.W. Hanson used 35
infrastructure factors and got 8,000 to 12,000 people; the older 30,000 to 45,000 figure would
mean 400 people per hectare, which he calls unsustainable ([Berks-Pompeii]).

**2. Density varies by culture, but a few bands repeat.**
- Medieval Europe: J.C. Russell: "The average population density of cities was about 100–120
  persons to the hectare. The densely populated city might run past 200 to the hectare but this
  was rare." (quoted in a review of Bowman and Wilson 2011, [Douban-Wilson]).
- Roman Mediterranean: "normal outer ranges of 100–400 people per hectare and likely ranges of
  150–250 people per hectare" ([Douban-Wilson]; the review places this in the discussion of
  Andrew Wilson's chapter but does not explicitly credit it to him). Hanson and Ortman found Roman
  cities ranging from 50 to 500 persons/ha (cited in Smith et al. 2020, [Smith-Maya]). Hanson's
  urban geography uses a sliding scale from 100 persons/ha for sites under 50 ha up to 500
  persons/ha for sites over 400 ha ([AJA-Hanson]).
- Small Roman town in Britain: Silchester, revised estimate about 5,500 people at about 130 per
  hectare, assuming 5 people per dwelling ([Silchester]).
- Islamic cities: the "From Baghdad to London" data set assumed 150 inhabitants per hectare for a
  medieval city, and about 75 for "garden" cities such as Baghdad, Basra and Sanaa; Sanaa was
  estimated at about 60 per hectare (425 ha, about 25,000 people) ([Baghdad-London]). This is a
  modelling assumption, not a measurement.
- Mughal India: Shahjahanabad (Delhi) was built for 60,000 people on about 7.12 km²
  ([Sahapedia]). That is about 84 people per hectare as a design figure (my arithmetic). A second
  source gives 569 ha (unverified, search summary only).
- Mesoamerica: Tenochtitlan-Tlatelolco, 212,500 people on 1,350 ha, about 157 per hectare (my
  arithmetic from Smith 2005 as tabulated by Sluyter, [LSU-Sluyter]). Tzintzuntzan: 30,000 on
  674 ha, about 45 per hectare. Zacapu: 20,000 on 1,100 ha, about 18 per hectare. Santa Rita
  Corozal: 7,000 on 500 ha, 14 per hectare (all my arithmetic from [LSU-Sluyter]).
- Classic Maya: urban cores only 10–20 persons/ha; surrounding urban zone 5–10, periurban 2–5,
  rural under 2 (Canuto et al. 2018 as cited in Smith et al. 2020, [Smith-Maya]).
- A modern outlier for comparison: the Fez medina in the late 1970s had nearly 180,000 people at
  650 per hectare ([IEMed-Fez]). This is 20th-century overcrowding, not a medieval value.

**3. Bigger towns are denser.**
Cesaretti et al. (2016) measured 173 European towns around AD 1300 (England, France and Belgium,
Germany, northern Italy). Settled area grows with population to the power 0.714 for all towns
(95% range 0.662 to 0.766). By region: France and Belgium 0.790, England 0.730, Germany 0.754,
northern Italy 0.720. The intercept for all towns is -2.125 (natural log) ([PMC5051806]).
The area they measured includes suburbs outside the walls and empty land inside them. Most of
their towns had between 2,000 and 10,000 people. The same scaling idea holds in the pre-Hispanic
Basin of Mexico (Ortman and colleagues, cited in [Smith-Maya]).

**4. Plot sizes (medieval England).**
- Post-Conquest town charters set burgage plots at 3 or 3.5 perches (16 or 18 m) wide by 12
  perches (60 m) deep, about a quarter acre ([Dodson-Burton], citing Grenville, *Medieval
  Housing*, 1997).
- Salisbury (1225): uniform plots 3 by 7 perches. Sherborne (1227–28): three sizes, 4 x 20,
  4 x 24 and 2 x 4 perches ([Dodson-Burton], citing Hoskins).
- Alnwick: Conzen's 1960s survey found most properties "still roughly 28 feet wide, or an obvious
  fraction or multiple of 28 feet" ([Alnwick-Civic]). 28 ft is about 8.5 m.
- Width to length ratio "often about 1:6" in Northumberland ([Northumberland-Archives]).
- Burton-in-Lonsdale surviving burgage fields: one about 30 m wide (1 acre), another about 18 m
  wide ([Dodson-Burton]).
- Game source (S. John Ross, not scholarship): shops often a single perch (about 16 ft, 5 m) wide
  but very deep; frontage units ranged from 10 to 24 ft earlier and settled near 16 ft later
  ([MDME]).

**5. Plot sizes (other cultures).**
- French bastides: plots 8 m frontage by 24 m deep, blocks (*gaches*) split lengthwise into two
  halves (*ayrals*) (unverified; Vikidia, a children's wiki, returned 403). Monpazier, the
  textbook bastide, measures about 400 m by 220 m and its houses were originally all the same
  size, separated by narrow alleys (*androns*) ([FrenchMoments-Monpazier]).
- Yuan Beijing (Dadu): each household was allotted eight *mu* of land for a residence
  ([Wiki-Siheyuan]). One *mu* is about 667 m² in modern use, so 8 mu is about 5,300 m²
  (conversion unverified; the Yuan *mu* may differ). Hutongs (lanes) about 30 ft (9 m) wide,
  spaced about 250 ft (76 m) apart, "about the width of a normal siheyuan" ([Syracuse-Yang]).
  Another source gives Dadu avenues 36 m, streets 18 m, hutongs 9.3 m, and siheyuan depth 83 m
  (unverified; page returned 410).
- Kyoto machiya: narrow frontage and deep body, nicknamed "eel's bed" (*unagi no nedoko*)
  ([Kyoto-Machiya-Inn], [Muza-chan]). Typical frontage 5.4 to 6 m and depth 20 m or more
  (unverified; source returned 403). Popular sources say Edo-period tax was proportional to
  frontage ([Kyoto-Machiya-Inn], [Muza-chan]). Professor Yoshifumi Muneta says the tax came
  after the houses were built, so the tax did not cause the shape (unverified; both pages
  carrying the lecture returned errors).
- Roman Rome: the 4th-century Regionary catalogues list about 42,000–46,000 *insulae* (apartment
  blocks) against about 1,790 *domus* (single-family houses) ([Wiki-Insula]). Lo Cascio reads
  *insula* as any freestanding building, not just housing, so dividing population by insulae is
  unsafe (unverified; BMCR review not fetched).

**6. Buildings: types, heights and how plots fill up.**
- Conzen's **burgage cycle**: "the progressive filling-in with buildings of the backland of
  burgages, terminating in the clearing of buildings and a period of urban fallow prior to the
  initiation of a redevelopment cycle" (Whitehand 2001, [Whitehand-Conzen]). In short: a new plot
  has one house at the street; over decades the back fills with workshops, extra dwellings and
  outbuildings; at the peak it is crammed; then it is cleared and lies empty ("urban fallow")
  until rebuilt.
- Plots were also split and merged. At Alnwick, plots were subdivided as population grew and
  combined as ownership changed, but only "a limited amount of dividing and combining was
  practical" ([Alnwick-Civic]).
- Excavated English example (Higham Ferrers, Northamptonshire): plots laid out in the 12th
  century; stone buildings on the frontage by the later 13th century; the back plots held only
  pits and ditches; a ditch and later a wall divided frontage from back ([HER-Higham]).
- Alnwick's 28-ft frontage is linked to the span of timber beams ([Alnwick-Civic]); a ~30 ft
  frontage allowed a two-bay building facing the street (unverified; Northumberland conservation
  appraisal PDF not fetched).
- Roman insulae could reach nine storeys until Augustus capped height at 70 Roman feet (20.7 m);
  Trajan lowered it to 60 Roman feet (17.75 m) ([Wiki-Insula]).
- Game source: medieval wood and half-timber buildings typically stand 1–3 storeys
  ([MDME]).
- Islamic courtyard house: in Fustat (old Cairo) the excavated houses centre on a courtyard with a
  T-shaped hall (*majlis*) (unverified; MIT course page not fetched). No floor areas found.

**7. Share of town area by use.**
Not found as percentages for any culture. The only measured figure: Tenochtitlan's epicentre
(temples, plazas) was 16.9 ha out of 1,350 ha, about 1.25% (my arithmetic, [LSU-Sluyter]). For
Edo, a search summary says townspeople (*chōnin*) were 44% of the population on 18% of the land
(unverified; source was a wiki diff page not fetched).

### Population to area: rules of thumb

1. **Fixed density (simplest).** `area_ha = population / density`. Pick density by culture:
   medieval Europe 100–120 (Russell, [Douban-Wilson]); Roman 150–250 ([Douban-Wilson]);
   compact Islamic 150, garden Islamic 60–75 ([Baghdad-London]); Aztec island city about 157
   ([LSU-Sluyter]); Maya-style dispersed 10–20 in the core ([Smith-Maya]).
   Example: 10,000 people at 110 /ha gives about 91 ha (my arithmetic).

2. **Size-dependent density (better).** Cesaretti et al. fit `ln(area) = -2.125 + 0.714 ln(pop)`
   for 173 European towns c.1300 ([PMC5051806]). Assumption: area is in hectares (the paper's
   examples are in hectares, but the fetched text does not state the regression's unit). Worked
   values (my arithmetic, `area = 0.119 * pop^0.714`):

   | Population | Area (ha) | Density (/ha) |
   |---|---|---|
   | 2,000 | ~27 | ~74 |
   | 10,000 | ~86 | ~117 |
   | 50,000 | ~270 | ~185 |

   These land inside Russell's 100–120 band at 10,000 people, which is a useful cross-check.
   The area includes suburbs and empty land inside walls.

3. **Hanson's Roman sliding scale.** 100 /ha for sites under 50 ha, rising to 500 /ha for sites
   over 400 ha ([AJA-Hanson]); intermediate steps not found.

4. **Household route.** `households = population / household_size`, with 3.5–5 per household in
   medieval England ([ADS-Bailey]) and about 3.8 in a large Italian city ([Medievalists-family]).
   Then plots ≈ households, minus shared tenements. This gives a plot count to fill.

5. **Game source (label: not scholarship).** S. John Ross, *Medieval Demographics Made Easy*:
   150 /ha average, 75–300 extremes, random density = sum of 7d4 drop the two highest, times 15;
   15–25 dwellings per acre (~50 /ha); ~3 people per dwelling; 1–2 buildings per dwelling; a
   10,000-person city covers about 67 ha inside the walls ([MDME]). His references include
   Russell's *Medieval Regions and Their Cities*. His 150 /ha is higher than Russell's 100–120.

### What this means for a generator

- **Density as a rule-pack parameter, by culture.** Store a density band per culture in the rule
  pack, not in code: e.g. medieval European 100–120, Roman 150–250, Islamic compact 150 / garden
  75, dispersed Mesoamerican 10–20 in the core. The seeded stream picks a value inside the band.
- **Use the scaling law, not a constant.** `area_ha = 0.119 * pop^0.714` (European c.1300) makes
  small towns sparse and big towns dense, matching the data. Keep the exponent and intercept as
  rule-pack numbers so other cultures can override them. The area includes suburbs; split into
  intra-mural and extra-mural only when a spec says the town is walled (GM? question if unknown).
- **Plots from a frontage grid.** European burgage blocks: frontage drawn from 1–3.5 perches
  (5–18 m), depth from about 35 m (Salisbury) to 60 m+ (charter standard), ratio about 1:6. Japan:
  narrow frontage and deep plot. Beijing: one courtyard lot spans the ~76 m between lanes. Plot
  widths should snap to a unit (perch, ken, etc.) and to fractions/multiples of it, as at Alnwick.
- **Model the burgage cycle as plot age.** A plot's build-up level grows with town age: street
  building only, then rear workshops and extra houses, then crammed, then cleared ("urban
  fallow"). The front strip is always built; the backland is garden or yard early and filled
  later. This is a deterministic function of plot age and seed, not of iteration order.
- **Household and building counts.** households = population / (3.5–5); buildings = 1–2 per
  dwelling (game source only, so mark it as such in the rule pack and do not treat it as
  calibrated).
- **Missing data stays explicit.** Coverage ratio per plot and land-use shares (streets, churches,
  open space) were not found. Leave them as tunable rule-pack values marked "uncalibrated", and
  calibrate later from measured maps (e.g. the town-plan corpus in `docs/research/`).
