### Notes per claim

**1. Cesaretti et al. 2016.** Everything in the claim matches Table 1: 173 cities, c. AD 1300, intercept -2.125 (so prefactor e^-2.125 = 0.119), exponent 0.714. The paper never states the area unit in Table 1. The unit is hectares, because the supplementary CSV lists Bristol at 130 and the text (p. 19) says "the estimate of 130 ha". Refitting the CSV with ordinary least squares gives 0.7141 and -2.1252, which match the table. The regional exponents are France and Belgium 0.790, England 0.730, Germany 0.754 and Northern Italy 0.720. In hectares: 2,000 people gives 27 ha, 10,000 gives 85 ha and 50,000 gives 270 ha.

**2. Russell.** I found no primary text of Russell that gives 100-120 per ha for medieval European towns. The only traceable "100-120 per ha" attributed to Russell is about **ancient** cities, from his 1977 Pompeii paper (via Van Limbergen & Vermeulen 2021, note 12). The popular "150 per ha" figure for medieval towns comes from S. John Ross's gaming guide *Medieval Demographics Made Easy*, not from Russell. The Cesaretti dataset is a primary, citable replacement: for 173 towns c. 1300 the median is 114 per ha, the mean 130, and the middle half of towns lies between 93 and 152.

**3. Roman densities.** The "150-250 (100-400)" band has no source I could find. The peer-reviewed relation is Hanson, Ortman & Lobo 2017, eq. 4.1: N = 41.834 x A^1.3361 (A in hectares). Density rises with town size, so a fixed band is misleading. Hanson 2016 used stepped classes from 100 to 500 per ha.

**4. Mesoamerica.** The Tenochtitlan figures are exactly Smith 2005, Table 5. The Maya "10-20 per ha core" fits only compact northern cities. The best-known Classic southern cities (Tikal, Caracol) are about 5 per ha across the mapped city.

**5. Bosker & Buringh.** The published paper and the 2012 working paper are different studies: different samples, model, base rate and Roman-road result. A design must cite one of them, not a blend. In the published version the base rate is 0.05% per century. The point effects are river +1.2, sea +0.7, road +0.4 and hub +6.9 or 7.3 (the authors themselves call the hub figure unreliable). Medium distance of 20-100 km gives +0.02 and under 20 km gives -0.05. The published paper also finds that first-nature geography (physical features) dominates throughout 800-1800. The working-paper claim that second-nature geography (position relative to existing cities) mattered only after 1600 is not in the published abstract.

**6. Motamed et al.** All four numbers match the working-paper text word for word ("slightly more than 8 and 18 years earlier"). The effects shrink at higher urbanisation thresholds (p. 13).

**7. Michaels & Rauch.** All numbers match CEP DP 1248. The 77% vs 52% compares the share of towns with coastal or navigable-river access in medieval Britain and in Roman Britain.
