## Early-medieval timber port towns

Scope: real towns c. 800-1200 that resemble the calibration city (Viking-age Dublin model):
Hiberno-Norse Dublin, Waterford, Wexford, Limerick, Viking York, Hedeby, Ribe, Birka, Kaupang,
Sigtuna, Trondheim, Bergen (Bryggen), Novgorod, Wolin, Truso. Nothing here is about the fictional
city. Every claim carries a source number from the Sources list. Each URL in that list was fetched
during this research. "(unverified)" means it came from a search snippet or a weak page and was not
confirmed on a reliable fetched page. "(Wikipedia)" marks a Wikipedia page. "(weak)" marks a page of
uncertain authorship.

Jargon, one line each:
- **Tenement / plot / burgage**: one long property strip owned or rented by one household.
- **Frontage**: the short side of a plot that touches the street.
- **Post-and-wattle**: walls of upright stakes with woven hazel rods between them.
- **Revetment**: a timber or stone facing that holds back earth at a bank or waterfront.
- **Land reclamation**: dumping fill behind a revetment so the shore moves out into the water.
- **Jetty / pier**: a timber platform reaching out over water for ships to tie up.
- **Longphort**: a 9th-century Viking ship camp in Ireland, defended on the land side.
- **Naust**: a boathouse, often a hollow dug into the shore with low side walls and a roof.
- **Dendrochronology**: dating timber from its tree-ring pattern, often to the exact year.
- **Emporium**: an early trading place, often seasonal at first, with a planned strip of plots.
- **Plan-unit**: a block of a town plan that was laid out at one time in one pattern.
- **Thing / thingmote**: a Norse assembly; the thingmote is the mound or place where it met.

### Findings

**1. Street pattern: one spine street along the shore, lanes down to the water.**
- Viking Dublin "had its long axis arranged east-west along High Street" on a ridge "some 15m high,
  running parallel to the river" [8]. The first plan-unit sat at a crossroads of an east-west street
  (Castle Street/Christ Church Place) and a north-south street (Fishamble Street/Werburgh Street) that
  ran down steep slopes to the river [8].
- Hedeby: streets laid out at right angles, running parallel to a stream [21]; east-west streets led
  down to jetties in the harbour [22 (Wikipedia)]; a boardwalk ran parallel to the bank with paths
  branching to the shore [19].
- Sigtuna: "All the houses in town face Stora gatan", the one main street; plots on each side of it
  [32, 33].
- Bergen: Øvregaten ("Strete") was probably the only street in the 1100s, parallel to the harbour; the
  tenements ran from it down to the water [30 (Wikipedia)].
- Trondheim: one north-south street bisected the plots on a narrow spit between river and bay [34,
  via search; see Not found]. Plots there had "the short side towards the street or towards the
  water" [34].
- Waterford: two main streets, roughly where Peter Street and High Street run today [12].

**2. Plots: long narrow strips, short side to the street, often running to the water or the bank.**
- Dublin, Fishamble Street: "trapezoidal plots, demarcated by post-and-wattle boundary fences. These
  plots narrowed towards the waterfront and had their widest ends along Fishamble Street" [3].
  Excavation on the west side of Fishamble Street found ten plots, "aligned to the modern street",
  broad end to the street, stretching back to the earthen embankment [8].
- Dublin elsewhere: "tiny rectangular plots, typically only slightly larger than the house itself",
  with "a much greater density of houses and population" than at Fishamble Street [6].
- York, Coppergate: four properties "defined by fence lines in c.AD 900"; buildings "arranged
  gable-ended onto the new street of Coppergate" [17]. The tenements ran from Coppergate back to the
  River Foss [15, 16].
- Sigtuna: plots "7-8 m wide and 30-40 m long", fixed size, laid out from scratch [32]; c. 180 plots,
  20 to 40 m long, separated by narrow alleys [33].
- Norwegian towns (Molaug): ground divided into plots first; "tenements are elongated with the short
  side towards the street or towards the water" [34].
- Ribe: an 8th-century market area "planned from the outset and divided into plots about 8 m wide"
  (unverified: search snippet of Feveile's paper, the PDF was blocked).
- Kaupang: first plots and structures laid out 780-800 [27].

**3. Plot boundaries persist for centuries.**
- Dublin, Fishamble Street: "A continuity of property lines from the 10th through to the 11th century"
  [3]. Fenced plots traceable "over a dozen successive building levels" [10b].
- Dublin, High Street: plot boundaries "did not stabilise until the mid eleventh century" (a later,
  piecemeal extension) [8].
- Waterford: plot orientation showed continuity over time, though fences were not always found [12];
  at least seven houses superimposed on one site between the 11th and mid-12th century [12].
- Wexford, Bride Street: a property split into two holdings in the early 12th century, and "that
  division lasted to the present day" [13].
- Ribe: the plot system "was maintained with only small changes throughout the 8th century"
  (unverified: Feveile snippet).
- Trondheim: wider riverside plots of the earliest period "were later divided" [34].

**4. Houses: small, rectangular, one per plot, rebuilt in place.**
- Dublin Type 1 (Wallace): rectangular, post-and-wattle, three aisles, central hearth, four main roof
  posts [2, 4-note]; about three quarters of all known house remains of the period [1]. Doors at each
  end; raised side areas for seats or beds; stone-kerbed hearth [10b].
- Fishamble Street 10th-century houses "averaged about 9m by 6m"; 11th-century houses "much smaller",
  with low side walls and steep roofs; single doors usually at the west end [3].
- Fishamble Street held over 120 houses on one site [2]; Wood Quay site overall over 100-200
  buildings across the dig [1, 2].
- Waterford: three-aisled houses with clay floors and wattle walls close to the street [12]. Houses
  faced the street "but not in a uniform modern-style row", and some frontages had no house [12].
- Wexford: fifteen post-and-wattle houses, early 11th to 13th/14th century, "a local variant" of
  Dublin Type 1 [13].
- Limerick: sunken-floored dwelling "in the Hiberno-Norse tradition" at King John's Castle, before
  the castle [14].
- York, Coppergate: early 10th-century post-and-wattle structures on each property, then semi-sunken
  plank-built structures for about 50 years, then surface-built [17]. Building size about 7 m x 4.5 m
  (unverified: school-lesson snippet).
- Hedeby: hall houses with footprints "between 3.5 x 17 m and 7 x 17.5 m" [23 (Wikipedia, German)].
  Typical dwelling rectangular with two or three rooms [21].
- Birka: radar survey found "small, tightly packed workshops" inside the rampart, none over about
  20 m2; larger longhouses outside the rampart [25 (Wikipedia, Swedish)].
- Truso: buildings c. 5 x 10 m and long houses about 6 x 21 m [38 (Wikipedia)].

**5. Streets and paths: narrow, surfaced with wattle, planks, logs or stones.**
- Dublin: access by pathways that "usually consisted of flat horizontal screens" (wattle mats) laid
  along the boundary fences [3].
- Waterford: an excavated section of Peter Street "over 2.5 m wide", surface of closely packed small
  stones [12].
- Wolin: post houses along a "1.55 m-wide street" (unverified: search snippet; the Janowski PDF [37]
  confirms streets passing over bridges to the quay only in a search snippet, not in the extracted
  text).
- Novgorod: wooden street paving first laid in the mid-10th century, relaid "at intervals of 20-30
  years" for six centuries, each layer dated by tree rings [36 (blog, secondary)]. Homesteads fenced by
  a palisade abutting the street pavement (unverified: search snippet of a 2011 excavation report).

**6. Waterfront: the shore moves outward in steps; jetties, not quays, at first.**
- Dublin: Waterfront I, an early 10th-century low bank (perhaps a flood bank); Waterfront II, a more
  substantial embankment "across the waterfront from east to west", protected by posts-and-wattle;
  Waterfront III "further out in the river bed later in the 10th century" in four stages; Waterfront
  IV, a stone wall further out, probably end of the 11th century [3]. Timber revetments then advanced
  northward; a stone quay in the mid-13th century [1]. Land north of Cook Street was reclaimed from the
  Liffey in the early 13th century [8].
- Bergen: original shoreline lay 100-150 m inside today's quay front; tenements gradually extended
  out into the harbour on timber caissons ("karverk") filled with stone [30 (Wikipedia, Norwegian)].
- Hedeby: jetties from the late 8th century [21]; large pier structures from the 860s [24]; jetty
  platforms "also served as a market place" [19]; harbour basin about 11 ha [23] or about 8 ha [24]
  (sources differ).
- Wolin: three piers "perpendicularly to the river channel", each an earth embankment faced with
  timber; one c. 20 m long and 15 m wide with a building on it; later a 1.5 m wide jetty added [37].
  Quay plus jetty length estimated at 250-300 m, serving "a dozen or even several dozen ships"; at
  its peak perhaps one harbour along almost 1,000 m of shore [37]. Trade "directly from the boats
  moored to the pier" [37].
- Sigtuna: no traces of jetties found; road access mattered more than water access early on [32].
- Kaupang: one jetty dated c. 803 [27].

**7. Shipbuilding sits on the shore, at or outside the town edge.**
- Birka: a shipyard at Kugghamn, "outside Birka's town rampart" on the northern shore: a stone-lined
  hollow with a wooden boat slip, boat rivets, whetstones, woodworking tools [26].
- Dublin: ship timbers, sketches on planks, model ships show shipbuilding by Scandinavian methods
  [10b]; oak chronologies show Dublin as a major shipbuilding centre [1]. The great longship Skuldelev
  2 (29.28 m long) was built of timber felled in 1042 near Dublin and Waterford, most likely in
  Dublin [11b]. The exact site of Dublin's yards: not found.
- Hedeby: royal longship Haithabu I built locally around 982, burned at its moorings [24].
- Wolin: ship parts reused to strengthen a pier [37].
- Boathouses (nausts): Harre Vig, Denmark, two foreshore boathouses c. 24 x 6 m inside, 11th century;
  Nergården, Norway, a 29 x 6 m Viking Age boathouse (both unverified: search snippets only).

**8. Fishing.**
- Dublin: many marine species eaten; lead line-weights, net-floats and sinkers found; small animal
  pens for pigs or goats by houses; cattle brought in from the countryside [9 (weak)].
- Hedeby: fishing gear and fishbones in the harbour [24].
- Fish-drying grounds or net yards as mapped zones: not found.

**9. Defences: bank-and-palisade on the land side; the waterfront is open or only lightly banked.**
- Ribe: in the first half of the 9th century "a 2-3 m wide trench and a low rampart were constructed in
  a semicircle around the eastern side"; the river was the natural western border; more a legal
  boundary than a fortification; turned into a proper moat in the late 10th century [29]. The major
  earthwork (rampart plus a moat about 20 m wide, over half a kilometre long) dates to the 10th
  century [28].
- Hedeby: semicircular rampart [19, 20], earthworks on the north, west and south sides [22
  (Wikipedia)]; the east side faced the harbour. Rampart about 9 m high, enclosing about 26 ha, built
  after 930 [23]; second half of the 10th century per the museum [19].
- Birka: an earthen rampart around the "black earth" town, ending at the hillfort Borgen in the south
  [25]. The rampart was also "a legal, economic and social boundary" (search snippet, unverified).
- Kaupang: "curiously appears not to have been fortified" [27].
- Dublin: earthen bank topped by a palisade in the 10th century, built of clay, river mud and refuse,
  reinforced with post-and-wattle or planks [9 (weak)]. A second, larger bank in the 11th century
  [10b]. South side at Christchurch Place: from "a simple, low counterscarp bank, less than 0.75m in
  height" to "a raised earthen mound almost 4m in height, and c. 5m in width", then a 12th-century
  stone wall about 3.5 m high [6b]. Werburgh Street: clay bank surviving 0.9 m high, c. 5 m wide;
  then a limestone wall 1.7 m wide "tentatively identified as Viking", c. 1100 [5]. Dublin's banks
  also ran along the waterfront (Waterfront II above) [3], so Dublin was not open on the river side.
- Dublin grew: the enclosed town expanded "eastwards towards the Poddle" [7]; High Street was a second
  plan-unit added to the west, its earliest levels c. 1010-1030 [8]; a stone wall c. 1100 "tripled the
  enclosed area" [9 (weak)].
- Waterford: ditch "up to 3m deep and 8m wide", bank up to 6 m wide, revetted by a stone wall 1.8 m
  wide; ditch timbers dated c. 1070-1080 [11]. The river Suir bounds the town on the north [12].
- York: Vikings withdrew behind the old Roman walls in 867 [15c (Wikipedia)].
- Wolin: wood-and-earth fortifications (search snippet); an Arab traveller wrote of "12 gates and a
  harbour built of cut tree trunks" (a written source of mixed reliability, quoted in [37]).

**10. Elite, assembly, churches, crafts.**
- Dublin: the oldest part (first plan-unit) was also the wealthiest; High Street was "an area of large
  yards with small dwellings and workshops", probably artisans [8]. Christ Church cathedral founded
  1038 [9 (weak)]. Essex Street was an industrial zone with thick charcoal and ash deposits into the
  11th-12th centuries [2]. The Steine, a standing stone marking the longship landing place, and the
  Thingmote stood east of the town at Hoggen Green near today's College Green [18 (Wikipedia)].
  Graves of the 9th-10th centuries at Kilmainham and Islandbridge, west of the town [10b].
- Hedeby: workshops for iron and glass working in the north-west; a large cemetery in the south-west
  [19].
- Birka: hillfort Borgen at the south end of the rampart [25]; administrative centre supposedly on
  the neighbouring island of Adelsö [24b (Wikipedia)]; nearly 3,000 graves, large grave fields outside
  the rampart [25].
- Kaupang: the Skiringssal hall just inland from the fjord [27b (Wikipedia)].
- Sigtuna: "A large plot of land in the centre of Sigtuna indicates the location of the royal seat",
  with a church and mint nearby [32]; six or seven stone churches later [33].
- York: possible royal residence "Konungsgurtha" near the east gate of the Roman fortress [15c].
- Comb-making is recorded at Dublin [10b], Ribe [20b], Hedeby [21] and Wexford [13]; craft zones at the
  edge for fire risk: not found as a stated rule.

**11. Surroundings.**
- Dublin: at the junction of "three major long-distance land routes", at a fordable point, close to
  Dublin Bay [8]. Hinterland "Dyflinnar skíði" reached roughly Wicklow, Leixlip and Skerries
  [18b (Wikipedia)]. Suburbs north of the river and along Thomas Street, Patrick Street and others were
  13th-century, not Viking [8].
- Birka: longhouses outside the rampart [25]; shipyard and a boat landing outside the rampart [26].
- Hedeby: ox-cart portage of about 16-18 km to Hollingstedt [23].
- Fisher hamlets outside the bank: not found.

### Case studies

1. **Dublin (Wood Quay, Fishamble Street).** Refounded 917 on a 15 m ridge along the Liffey above the
   Poddle confluence [8]. Spine street along the ridge, cross street down to the river [8].
   Trapezoidal fenced plots, wide end on the street, narrowing to the waterfront bank [3, 8]. Type 1
   houses about 9 x 6 m [3]. Banks on the river side as well as inland, pushed out in stages [3].
   Extended west (High Street, c. 1010-1030) and east (towards the Poddle) [7, 8]. Walled area c. 12 ha
   [8]. Thingmote and Steine landing stone outside, to the east [18].
2. **Waterford.** River Suir on the north [12]. Two main streets [12]. Ditch 8 m wide with stone-faced
   bank, c. 1070-1080 [11]. Houses rebuilt in place up to seven times [12].
3. **Wexford.** Fifteen post-and-wattle houses facing the medieval waterfront; crafts include
   ironworking, combs, shoes, querns; a 12th-century plot split still visible today [13].
4. **Limerick.** Settled 922, on a defensible site near the Shannon and Abbey River [10c].
   Archaeology thin; sunken-floored Hiberno-Norse dwellings under King John's Castle [14].
5. **York, Coppergate.** Fenced plots from c. 900, gable-ended to the new street, running back to the
   River Foss [15, 17]. Crafts: smithing, metalwork, woodturning, dyeing, weaving, coin dies [17].
6. **Hedeby.** Semicircular rampart on the land side, harbour on the east [19, 22]. Right-angled
   streets along a stream [21]. Jetties grew into platforms used as the market [19, 20]. Workshops
   north-west, cemetery south-west [19].
7. **Ribe.** Plots along the river; a low ditch-and-bank semicircle on the land (east) side with the
   river as the western edge [29]; 10th-century moat c. 20 m wide [28].
8. **Birka.** 7 ha black earth inside a rampart ending at a hillfort [25]. Tiny workshops inside,
   longhouses, shipyard and graves outside [25, 26].
9. **Kaupang.** c. 5 ha beach settlement, plots laid out 780-800, a jetty c. 803, no defences [27].
10. **Sigtuna.** Planned c. 980; c. 180 plots 7-8 m wide on both sides of one main street; large
    central royal plot with church and mint; no jetties found [32, 33].
11. **Bergen (Bryggen).** Rows of timber buildings in tenements between the one street and the
    harbour; the shoreline moved 100-150 m out over centuries on stone-filled timber caissons [30].
12. **Wolin.** Earth-and-timber piers set at right angles to the river, a 250-300 m harbour front,
    trade straight from boats [37].
