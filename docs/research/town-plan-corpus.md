# Town-plan corpus for expressive-range analysis

> Written 2026-10-19 by a research agent. A claim here is binding only when a DECISIONS.md
> entry cites it. Population figures are approximate; see the conventions below.

Research report, 2026-10-19. Purpose: a shortlist of real pre-industrial town plans whose
geometry can be measured (by hand or by tracing) and compared with generated cities, so we can
see whether the generator covers the real range of wall shapes, gate counts, market placement,
street-pattern types and block sizes.

Conventions used below:

- **Population figures are approximate.** They come from general historical literature and
  are not individually verified in this session; treat them as order-of-magnitude. Where a
  figure is especially shaky it is marked *(unverified)*.
- **Geometry facts** are read off the cited plan plus standard historical topography. A bird's
  eye view by Braun and Hogenberg or Speed is a *picture*, not a survey: street widths and
  block counts are indicative, gate counts and wall shape are reliable, relative positions of
  market, church and river are reliable.
- All Wikimedia Commons links were checked through the Commons API on 2026-10-19 for pixel
  size and licence tag. BnF (Gallica) uploads are tagged public domain; David Rumsey uploads
  (Internet Archive "dr_" identifiers) are tagged public domain on Commons and CC BY-NC-SA on
  Rumsey's own site, so cite Commons.
- The URLs are Commons *file pages*; the original-resolution image is the "Original file"
  link on each page.

## 2. Town notes

### 2.1 Dublin, 1610 (John Speed)

- Plan: inset on *The Countie of Leinster with the citie Dublin described*, 1610, Speed,
  engraved by Hondius. The BnF scan above shows the whole county sheet; the Dublin inset is
  the lower-left corner at roughly 1500 px wide, which is enough to trace. A second BnF copy
  (`btv1b532254353`) exists if the first is damaged. Commons also holds an 1896 Norwegian
  redrawing (`File:Dublin 1610 1896.jpg`, 1075 x 927) that is cleaner for reading street
  lines.
- Population c. 1610: roughly 10,000-15,000 (civic sources give c. 10,000 for the medieval
  town, c. 21,000 by 1640).
- Geometry:
  - One wall ring, roughly D-shaped, flat side on the River Liffey (north edge). Walled area
    about 20 ha. Short river frontage, long landward arc.
  - Gates: six to seven principal (Dame's Gate E, Newgate W, St Nicholas' Gate S, Pole Gate S,
    Bridge Gate N, plus posterns); one stone bridge, at the west end of the river frontage.
  - Castle in the south-east corner, on the wall line, straddling the Poddle tributary; the
    cathedral (Christ Church) is central on the ridge, the second cathedral (St Patrick's) is
    *outside* the wall to the south.
  - Market and High Street run east-west along the ridge between Dame's Gate and Newgate;
    the main spine is the market street. Corn market just inside Newgate.
  - Street pattern: organic but with a dominant east-west spine and lanes running downslope
    north to the quays; blocks are long, thin and perpendicular to the spine (burgage pattern).
  - Suburbs: very large relative to the walled core. Oxmantown across the bridge (the Viking
    quarter), St Thomas's suburb west, the Liberties south around St Patrick's, a ribbon east
    to Trinity College. On Speed's inset the extramural built-up area roughly equals the
    intramural.
  - Terrain: the walled town sits on a low ridge above the tidal river; no cliff, but a clear
    slope down to the quays.

### 2.2 Galway, c. 1651 (anonymous pictorial map)

- Plan: the "Pictorial Map of Galway", drawn c. 1651 (dating disputed; may be post-1660),
  surviving in two printed copies (Trinity College Dublin; James Hardiman Library, NUI
  Galway). The NUI Galway copy is digitised and released CC BY, but only a 600 x 891 image is
  exposed on the aggregator; the library's own viewer (`archives.library.nuigalway.ie/citymap/`)
  did not respond when checked. The Royal Irish Academy volume *Renaissance Galway* (Walsh,
  2019) reproduces the TCD copy at high resolution but is not openly licensed. The Commons
  file `Old-Galway.jpg` is a 947 x 668 public-domain redrawing, adequate for topology only.
- Population c. 1650: perhaps 5,000-7,000 *(unverified)*.
- Geometry:
  - Single wall ring, roughly rectangular with the long axis north-south, sitting on the east
    bank where the River Corrib meets the sea. River on the west, tidal harbour on the south.
  - About 14 towers, five or six gates; the principal gates are the Great Gate (east/landward,
    on the road to Dublin), the West Bridge Gate across the Corrib, and the quay gates south.
  - Market (the market cross and St Nicholas' collegiate church) sits in the centre of the
    walled area; the church is one block from the principal cross-street. This is the clearest
    "church-and-market in the middle, gates at the ends" example in the corpus.
  - Street pattern: near-grid. Two or three parallel north-south streets crossed by three
    east-west streets, a legacy of Anglo-Norman planning; blocks are rectangular and quite
    small (c. 60-80 m).
  - Suburbs: small; the map shows the Claddagh fishing village across the river mouth as a
    separate, unwalled, organic cluster, which is exactly the "fishing village beside the
    walled town" pattern.
  - Water: river, bridge, and quays on two sides; one landward side with the main gate.

### 2.3 Visby, c. 1590-1598 (Braun and Hogenberg)

- Plan: *Visbia Gothorum*, Civitates Orbis Terrarum vol. 5 (plate 39). The Commons file is an
  11185 x 7546 CC0 scan from a Swedish institution. Shows the town after its medieval peak, so
  there are empty areas inside the wall.
- Population c. 1600: small, perhaps 2,000-3,000 *(unverified)*; the wall was built for a
  much larger 13th-century town.
- Geometry:
  - Single wall ring, 3.4 km long, roughly a quarter-circle with the straight side on the
    sea (west) and a curved landward arc. The sea wall is partly lost; the landward wall has
    c. 27 surviving towers of an original c. 29 plus many wall-top turrets.
  - Gates: three main landward gates (Norderport, Österport, Söderport) plus small sea gates
    on the harbour; gate density is low for the wall length.
  - The harbour and market (Stora Torget) are both near the shore in the south-west; the
    cathedral (St Maria) is further north on the slope. Many ruined churches are scattered
    over the whole walled area, a reminder that church count scales with the 13th-century
    population, not the 16th.
  - Terrain: a cliff/terrace line (the "Klint") runs through the town parallel to the shore,
    splitting it into a lower harbour town and an upper plateau; streets climb it by ramps.
  - Street pattern: organic, with a loose tendency to run parallel and perpendicular to the
    shore; blocks are irregular and large (vacant lots).
  - Suburbs: none; extramural land is fields and the small fishing settlement at the harbour.

### 2.4 Bergen, 1581 (Hieronymus Scholeus)

- Plan: drawn 1581 by Scholeus, engraved for Civitates vol. 4 (1588). The Nasjonalmuseet
  copy on Commons is 13627 x 9913, public domain; the BnF copy (`btv1b532358549`, 5978 x 8401)
  is a 1635 printing. It is an oblique view from the sea, so distances along the depth axis
  are compressed; use it for topology and relative position, not for block measurement.
- Population c. 1600: roughly 10,000-15,000, the largest town in Norway.
- Geometry:
  - **No town wall.** Defence is by the royal castle/fortress (Bergenhus) on the headland at
    the mouth of the inner harbour (Vågen) and by the hills behind.
  - The town is a ribbon around the harbour: the Hanseatic wharf (Bryggen) on the east shore,
    the Norwegian town around the head and west shore, warehouses pushing into the water.
  - Timber construction throughout, long narrow tenements perpendicular to the quay (each a
    "gård" with a passage), so blocks are very elongated: c. 10-15 m wide by 100 m deep.
  - Market at the head of the harbour where the two shores meet, with the cathedral and the
    main churches slightly uphill from it.
  - Street pattern: linear along the shore plus short steep lanes climbing the hillside;
    the hills (Fløyen etc.) close off the landward side within a few hundred metres.
  - Fishing boats, drying frames and small boathouses are drawn all along the shore: a good
    reference for the "working waterfront" density of a fishing port.

### 2.5 Shrewsbury, 1610 (John Speed)

- Plan: inset on *Shropshyre described*, 1610; BnF scan 6872 x 5360. The inset is
  comparatively detailed (Speed surveyed some towns himself).
- Population c. 1610: roughly 5,000-7,000.
- Geometry:
  - The River Severn makes an almost closed loop; the town fills the loop. The wall follows
    the river bank and crosses the 300 m neck on the north-east, where the castle sits.
  - Two bridges, each on the opposite side (English Bridge east, Welsh Bridge west), each with
    a gate tower on the bridge and a suburb beyond (Abbey Foregate, Frankwell).
  - Gates: the two bridge gates plus the castle gate at the neck; a few posterns. Three
    principal entrances is the minimum you can have for a loop town.
  - Market square (and the two main churches, St Mary's and St Alkmund's) on the high ground in
    the centre of the loop; the streets converge on it from the two bridges and the castle gate,
    giving a triangular "three-spoke" radial pattern.
  - Street pattern: organic radial on a hump; blocks are irregular, medium sized, with
    "shuts" (alleys) cutting through.
  - Suburbs: two substantial bridgehead suburbs and one along the Abbey.

### 2.6 Durham, 1610 (John Speed, after Schwytzer 1595)

- Plan: inset on *The bishoprick and citie of Durham*; BnF scan 6800 x 5360. The British
  Library catalogue calls the inset "a stylised earlier bird's-eye view", so treat the street
  detail as approximate. The small Commons file `Durham 1610.jpg` is only 400 px.
- Population c. 1600: perhaps 3,000-5,000 *(unverified)*.
- Geometry:
  - Rocky peninsula with the River Wear wrapping three sides in a deep wooded gorge; cliffs
    of 20-30 m on the river sides.
  - The inner wall encloses only the plateau (castle + cathedral + Palace Green, c. 10 ha);
    the castle sits at the landward neck (north), the cathedral at the far (south) end. This is
    the "castle on the landward side of a peninsula" layout.
  - The market place is *outside* the plateau wall, just below the castle at the neck, where
    three streets (to the two bridges and north) meet. A second, outer wall line enclosed the
    market and the lower town.
  - Two medieval bridges (Framwellgate north-west, Elvet east) each with a suburb beyond; a
    third suburb (Gilesgate) on the landward ridge.
  - Street pattern: a single spine street down the peninsula from the neck to the cathedral,
    with the market as a knuckle at the neck; otherwise organic.
  - Blocks: long burgages on the Bailey streets; large institutional blocks (cathedral close)
    dominate the plateau.

### 2.7 Caernarfon, 1610 (John Speed)

- Plan: inset on *Caernarvon Both Shyre and shire-towne*; David Rumsey scan 12205 x 8282
  (public domain on Commons). Also in the Lilley/Lloyd/Trick GIS atlas (section 3).
- Population c. 1600: perhaps 1,000-1,500 *(unverified)*; the plantation was laid out for
  about 60-70 burgages.
- Geometry:
  - Walled town of c. 4 ha, roughly rectangular/trapezoid, built 1283-92 as an Edwardian
    bastide: one wall ring with 8 towers, the castle attached to the south side of the
    enclosure, sea (Menai Strait) on the west and the River Seiont on the south.
  - Two gates only: East Gate (landward, with the Exchequer over it) and West Gate (to the
    quay). The castle has its own gates.
  - Streets: a grid of two east-west streets and three north-south streets, blocks c. 50-60 m,
    burgage plots 18-24 m wide by 60 m deep. The church (St Mary's) is in the north-west corner
    built into the wall, not in the centre; the market was held on the street.
  - Suburb: the pre-conquest Welsh settlement and later growth lie east of the walls across
    the Cadnant stream.
  - This is the smallest and most regular town in the corpus: a useful lower bound.

### 2.8 Edinburgh, c. 1581 (Braun and Hogenberg)

- Plan: *Edenburgum Scotiae Metropolis*, Civitates vol. 3. British Museum copy on Commons,
  2500 x 1917, public domain. The National Library of Scotland offers a higher-resolution zoom
  (CC BY) at https://maps.nls.uk/view/00003284. An oblique from the south, so north-south
  distances are compressed.
- Population c. 1580: roughly 12,000-15,000 within the burgh; 20,000+ including Canongate and
  Leith.
- Geometry:
  - Ridge town: the castle sits on a volcanic crag at the west end, Holyrood Abbey/Palace at the
    east end, 1.6 km apart. The ridge drops steeply to the Nor' Loch (north) and the Cowgate
    valley (south).
  - One wall ring (Flodden Wall, 1513-60) enclosing a long oval c. 55 ha; the castle forms the
    western apex. Canongate, the eastern half of the ridge, is a separate unwalled burgh.
  - Gates (ports): about six (Netherbow E on the spine, West Port, Bristo Port, Potterrow
    Port, Cowgate Port, Kirk o' Field).
  - Main street (High Street) runs the whole ridge from castle to Netherbow and *is* the
    market; St Giles' (the main church) sits on it, mid-way, with the tolbooth and market cross
    beside it. Closes (alleys) descend the slopes at right angles every 10-20 m.
  - Street pattern: linear spine with herringbone closes, block depth up to 100 m; the tallest
    tenements in Europe at the time.
  - Suburbs: Canongate (planned linear burgh), Leith (port, 3 km away), scattered ribbon on the
    south roads.

### 2.9 Nuremberg, 1575 (Braun and Hogenberg)

- Plan: *Norenberga*, Civitates (1575). BnF scan 5881 x 8538, public domain. Merian's
  1648 plan (*Topographia Franconiae*; Commons `De Merian Frankoniae 100/101.jpg`,
  c. 3700 px) is a plan view, better for measuring.
- Population c. 1575: roughly 40,000 — the biggest city here; use only the core if you need a
  comparable size.
- Geometry:
  - Two successive wall rings are legible: the 11th-13th-century inner cores on each bank
    (St Sebald town north, St Lorenz town south, each with its own market), and the 14th-
    15th-century outer wall, 5 km long, nearly circular, with c. 80 towers and a wide dry moat.
  - Gates: four main gates on the outer wall (Laufer, Frauen, Spittler, Neutor), one roughly
    on each compass direction, plus posterns and water gates where the Pegnitz enters and exits.
  - The castle (Kaiserburg) sits on the sandstone rock at the north edge, on the wall line,
    highest point of the city.
  - The River Pegnitz crosses the city east-west with several bridges and islands; the main
    market (Hauptmarkt) is just north of the river in the Sebald half; each half has a
    parish church.
  - Street pattern: organic, with radial roads from the four gates converging on the river
    crossings; blocks small and irregular (30-60 m).
  - Suburbs: small, because the outer wall was generous; gardens and fields inside the wall
    in the north-east.

### 2.10 Nördlingen, 1643 (Matthäus Merian)

- Plan: *Topographia Sueviae* (1643). BnF scan 5100 x 3500, public domain; a 3200 px
  alternative is `File:Noerdlingen-1643-Merian.jpg`. A true bird's-eye plan with the wall
  circuit complete.
- Population c. 1640: roughly 6,000-9,000.
- Geometry:
  - Almost perfectly circular outer wall (c. 2.7 km, 1327 onwards), fully preserved today,
    with 5 gates (Baldinger, Löpsinger, Deininger, Reimlinger, Berger), 11-12 towers, a moat.
  - The earlier inner ring (c. 1200) is readable as a ring street around the core, so the town
    has two rings and the characteristic ring-road + radial pattern.
  - The market and the parish church (St Georg, with the "Daniel" tower) are at the centre of
    the circle; radial streets from each gate lead to it.
  - Street pattern: radial-concentric, the cleanest example of the type; blocks are wedge-
    shaped, 40-80 m.
  - River: the Eger runs outside the wall to the south; the town is on flat ground (Ries
    crater). No water inside except mill channels.
  - Suburbs: minimal in 1643 (the town had just been besieged in 1634).

### 2.11 Luxembourg, 1581 (Braun and Hogenberg, after Jacob van Deventer)

- Plan: *Lutzenburgum*, Civitates vol. 3 (1581). BnF scan of the 1645 printing, 5016 x 4208,
  public domain. A proper plan view. Blaeu's 1649 view (`File:Blaeu View of Luxembourg
  1649.jpg`) is an alternative.
- Population c. 1580: perhaps 6,000-10,000 *(unverified)*.
- Geometry:
  - Plateau town on a sandstone promontory (the Bock) with 40-60 m cliffs on three sides,
    at the confluence of the Alzette and the Pétrusse. The upper town fills the plateau; the
    lower towns (Grund, Clausen, Pfaffenthal) sit in the gorges below.
  - Walls: the plateau is walled on the open western/landward side (two successive medieval
    rings, the later Wenceslas wall of 1390 extending across the gorge to enclose the lower
    towns). By 1581 the landward side already has early bastions.
  - Gates: a few on the landward face; access from the valleys is by steep ramps and gates at
    the cliff foot.
  - The castle/count's seat is on the Bock rock at the *tip* of the promontory, i.e. on the
    river side, not the landward side; the market place and St Nicholas' church are in the
    middle of the plateau; the Benedictine abbey (Münster) is down in the Grund.
  - Cliff-face tunnels: the Bock casemates (17th-18th century, so not on this plan) are cut
    into the cliff below the castle — the real-world model for a cliff-face cave entrance.
  - Street pattern: organic on the plateau; the lower towns are linear along the river.

### 2.12 Siena, 1572 (Braun and Hogenberg)

- Plan: *Sena*, one of four panels on Civitates vol. 1 pl. 48 (Parma, Siena, Palermo,
  Trapani). David Rumsey scan 11083 x 7444, public domain; the Siena panel is about
  5000 px wide. Oblique view from the south-west.
- Population c. 1570: roughly 15,000-20,000 (down from 50,000 before the 1348 plague and the
  1555 siege).
- Geometry:
  - Hill town on three converging ridges forming a Y; the single wall (7 km, 14th century)
    follows the ridge contours, so the enclosed shape is a deeply lobed, irregular polygon
    with a lot of empty land (gardens, olive groves) inside.
  - Gates: eight principal gates (Camollia N, Romana SE, Pispini E, Ovile NE, Tufi S,
    San Marco SW, Fontebranda W, Laterina W), each at the end of a ridge road.
  - The Campo (shell-shaped market square) is at the junction of the three ridges, in the
    valley head between them; the cathedral is on the top of the western ridge above it. So
    market low at the junction, church high on a ridge.
  - Street pattern: the three main streets run along the ridge crests from the gates to the
    Campo; everything else is steep organic lanes falling off the crests. Blocks are very
    irregular; built density is highest within 200 m of the Campo.
  - Fortress (Medici Fortezza, 1560) on the north-west edge, on the wall line.
  - Suburbs: effectively none; the wall was oversized.

### 2.13 Toledo, 1566 (Joris Hoefnagel)

- Plan: *Toletum*, drawn by Hoefnagel 1566, published Civitates vol. 5 (1598). David Rumsey
  scan 11024 x 7546, public domain. Oblique from the north. The BnF copy (`btv1b53194460n`)
  is a 1566-1640 impression.
- Population c. 1566: roughly 50,000-60,000.
- Geometry:
  - Granite hill in a tight loop of the Tagus, which runs in a gorge 100 m below the city on
    the east, south and west. The only land approach is the north (landward) side, which has
    the full wall and the two monumental gates (Puerta de Bisagra, Puerta del Sol).
  - Two bridges, one each side of the loop (Alcántara E, San Martín W), each with a gate
    tower.
  - The Alcázar (fortress) sits on the highest point at the east, on the river side; the
    cathedral is in the middle of the hill; the main market square (Zocodover) is between
    them, near the Alcázar.
  - Street pattern: dense Islamic-medieval labyrinth — short, bent, dead-end alleys
    (adarves), very small blocks (15-40 m), covered passages. The CSTB Toledo study (section 3)
    gives street widths of 2-5 m.
  - Suburbs: the Arrabal to the north inside the outer (Islamic) wall; mills and the
    Antequeruela along the river.

### 2.14 Monpazier (founded 1284, planned bastide)

- Plan: no early-modern view exists; use (a) the 2013 aerial photograph by the mairie
  (3840 x 2160, CC BY-SA 4.0), which shows the whole grid in one frame, (b) Viollet-le-Duc's
  c. 1856 plan of the town (591 x 507, PD), and (c) the Napoleonic cadastre (c. 1830) held by
  the Archives départementales de la Dordogne, online but with no licence statement found in
  this session.
- Population: perhaps 1,500-2,500 in the 14th century *(unverified)*; c. 500 today.
- Geometry:
  - Rectangle 400 x 220 m, one wall ring (largely gone), three surviving gates of an original
    six (two on each short side, one on each long side mid-way).
  - Grid: 4 streets by 4 streets, 8 blocks of 2 columns of plots; blocks c. 45 x 100 m;
    plots 8 x 20 m, separated by 30 cm gaps (andrones) as fire breaks.
  - Central arcaded square occupies one full block; the church sits on the block diagonally
    adjacent (not on the square), the market hall is in the square. The square is central
    in the plan, the church off-centre.
  - Street hierarchy: three widths (carreyras 6-8 m, transversal streets, carreyrous 2 m
    alleys).
  - Terrain: plateau edge; no river inside the town; no suburbs of note.

### 2.15 Damascus, 1575 (Braun and Hogenberg)

- Plan: *Damascus, urbs nobilissima ad Libanum montem, totius Syriae metropolis*, Civitates
  vol. 2 (1575). BnF scan of the 1645 printing, 6472 x 4954, public domain. A
  Renaissance-European reconstruction, not a survey: the wall outline, gate count, river
  channels and citadel position are credible, the street grid is schematic.
- Population c. 1575: perhaps 50,000-60,000 *(unverified)*.
- Geometry:
  - Walled city roughly a rounded rectangle 1.5 x 0.8 km, the Roman-Hellenistic outline;
    one wall ring with a moat fed by the river.
  - The Barada river splits into channels running along and through the north side; the city
    is an oasis town (the Ghouta gardens) on the edge of the desert, so every extramural area
    is irrigated garden, not fields.
  - Seven classical gates (Bab Sharqi E, Bab Tuma NE, Bab al-Salam N, Bab al-Faradis N,
    Bab al-Jabiya W, Bab al-Saghir S, Bab Kisan SE).
  - Citadel in the north-west corner on the wall line, next to the river, flat ground.
  - The great mosque (Umayyad) is in the centre-west; the main souqs run as covered linear
    markets from the west gate to the mosque (the "Straight Street" east-west spine).
  - Street pattern: one straight east-west spine and organic residential labyrinths off it,
    with blind alleys and courtyard houses; blocks small and irregular.
  - Suburbs: large (Midan south, Salihiyya north on the mountain slope), linear along roads.

## 3. Scholarly sources on quantitative town-form measures

1. **Conzen, M. R. G. (1960), *Alnwick, Northumberland: a study in town-plan analysis*,
   Institute of British Geographers Publication 27.** The origin of "town-plan analysis":
   decomposes a plan into streets/street-system, plots/plot-pattern, and building block-plans;
   defines plan units, fringe belts, the burgage cycle, and metrological analysis of plot
   widths. No open-access edition; a good open summary is the EPUM briefing paper
   https://www.cs.ucy.ac.cy/projects/epum/?p=379 and Oliveira's review at
   https://vitoroliveira.fe.up.pt/06-historico-bl.pdf. Cheap metrics: plot-width histogram
   along a street (metrological analysis), ratio of plot depth to width, number of distinct
   plan units (regions with homogeneous street/plot orientation).

2. **Craane, M. (2009), "The medieval urban movement economy: using space syntax in the study
   of medieval towns as exemplified by 's-Hertogenbosch"**, Proceedings 7th International
   Space Syntax Symposium, Stockholm,
   https://www.spacesyntax.net/wp-content/uploads/symposia/SSS7_proceedings.pdf; and Craane,
   "Analysing medieval urban space; a methodology", *Internet Archaeology* 21 (2007),
   https://www.intarch.ac.uk/journal/issue21/5/index.html (open access). Applies Conzen plan
   reconstruction plus axial-map integration to Dutch medieval towns and tests whether markets
   sit on the most integrated streets. Cheap metrics: axial-line count, mean connectivity,
   global integration (closeness on the axial graph), and the integration rank of the street
   the market sits on. Note the stated bias: integration favours long straight streets, so
   curved medieval streets must be segmented consistently.

3. **Louf, R. and Barthelemy, M. (2014), "A typology of street patterns", *J. R. Soc.
   Interface* 11: 20140924**, open preprint https://arxiv.org/abs/1410.2094. Measures city
   blocks (faces of the street graph) by area and by shape factor Φ = block area / area of
   circumscribed circle; block area follows a power law with exponent about 2 in all 131
   cities, so the discriminating signal is the conditional distribution of Φ given area (the
   "fingerprint"). Cheap metrics: block-area distribution and Φ histogram; both are a few
   lines of polygon code and are exactly the kind of number a generator should be able to hit.

4. **Boeing, G. (2020), "A multi-scale analysis of 27,000 urban street networks", *Environment
   and Planning B* 47(4): 590-608**, preprint https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.02198, data on Harvard
   Dataverse. Defines the standard OSMnx street-network measures: intersection density, average
   street segment length, proportion of 4-way vs 3-way vs dead-end nodes, circuity (network
   distance / straight-line distance), orientation entropy (Boeing 2019, "Urban spatial order",
   https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.00600). Cheap metrics: node-degree histogram, mean segment
   length, circuity, orientation entropy — all computable from a planar graph in a few dozen
   lines; orientation entropy separates grid bastides from organic towns in one number.

5. **Dibble, J., Prelorendjos, A., Romice, O., Zanella, M., Strano, E., Pagel, M. and Porta, S.
   (2019), "On the origin of spaces: morphometric foundations of urban form evolution",
   *Environment and Planning B* 46(4): 707-730**, DOI 10.1177/2399808317725075, open repository
   copy https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/61294. Measures 207 variables on 45 "sanctuary
   areas" of medieval, 19th-century and post-war origin; nine suffice to classify by period
   (street-edge definition, building coverage, plots fronting streets). Companion:
   Fleischmann, Romice and Porta (2021), "Measuring urban form: overcoming terminological
   inconsistencies", *EPB* 48(8): 2133-2150, DOI 10.1177/2399808320910444, and the open-source
   momepy toolkit (https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/70616). Cheap metrics from their list:
   building coverage ratio per block, plot frontage ratio, block perimeter/area compactness,
   and mean block size per sanctuary area.

Also useful, not strictly quantitative: Lilley, Lloyd and Trick (2005), *Mapping the Medieval
Townscape: a digital atlas of the new towns of Edward I*, Archaeology Data Service,
https://archaeologydataservice.ac.uk/archives/view/atlas_ahrb_2005/ (DOI 10.5284/1000022) —
GIS reconstructions with downloadable data for Caernarfon, Conwy, Flint, Winchelsea and others,
i.e. ground-truth plot polygons for the bastide end of the range. Salat/CSTB, *The art of
medieval sustainable urban design: the case of Toledo*,
https://www.irbnet.de/daten/iconda/CIB17089.pdf, gives a measured 200 x 200 m sample of Toledo
(street widths 2-5 m, high coverage).

### Metrics that are cheap on a polygon map (recommended first set)

Given a generated `CityModel` with wall polygon(s), gate points, street segments and block
polygons, the following need no external library and no raster work:

| Metric | Source | Computation |
|---|---|---|
| Wall compactness | Conzen, Dibble | 4π·area / perimeter² of the wall polygon; count of rings |
| Gates per km of wall; gate angular spacing | this corpus | trivial |
| Market and main-church offset | Craane | distance from centroid of walled area, normalised by equivalent radius; also graph distance to nearest gate |
| Block-area distribution and shape factor Φ | Louf and Barthelemy | polygon area; circumscribed circle via minimum enclosing circle |
| Node-degree histogram, mean segment length, circuity | Boeing | planar street graph |
| Orientation entropy of street bearings | Boeing 2019 | histogram of segment bearings in 36 bins |
| Plot-width histogram on the main street | Conzen metrology | frontage lengths of lots touching the spine |
| Extramural / intramural built area ratio | this corpus | polygon areas |
| Water-frontage fraction of the wall | this corpus | wall edges within d of water polygon |

Space-syntax integration is also cheap (BFS on the axial or segment graph) but needs a
consistent axial-line construction first; defer it.

## 4. Recommendation: three towns for the three calibration cities

**Truro (Dublin-like timber fishing port, river, palisade landward, hills behind): use
Dublin 1610 as the primary reference and Bergen 1581 as the secondary.** Dublin gives the
walled-core-on-a-ridge-above-a-tidal-river layout, the single bridge, the castle on the
seaward corner and, importantly, suburbs as large as the core; Bergen supplies what Dublin
lacks — an unwalled timber waterfront with elongated quay tenements and hills closing the
landward side within a few hundred metres, which matches Truro's "palisade landward, hills
behind" better than any stone-walled English town. Galway c. 1651 is the fallback if a
near-grid interior is wanted, but its high-resolution scan is not openly available.
**Arcachon (walled stone capital on a peninsula, four compass-locked districts, castle on
the landward south side): use Durham 1610, with Toledo 1566 and Nuremberg 1575 as
secondaries.** Durham is the one town here with a castle sitting at the landward neck of a
cliff-edged river peninsula and the cathedral at the far tip; Toledo gives the large-capital
version (two bridges, monumental landward gates, fortress high on one side, dense interior)
and Nuremberg gives the "four gates on four compass points plus two halves with their own
markets" pattern that the compass-locked districts rule needs to reproduce. **Bearna
(cliff-top city at the meeting of three waters with a cliff-face cave entrance): use
Luxembourg 1581, with Siena 1572 as the secondary for the ridge-and-gorge interior.**
Luxembourg is a plateau city on 40-60 m cliffs at a river confluence, with lower towns in the
gorges, access ramps cut into the rock, and the later Bock casemates as a literal cliff-face
entrance; Siena shows how a wall and street system behave when they must follow three
converging ridges with the market at the junction, which is the right template for a
"three waters" valley system seen from the plateau.
