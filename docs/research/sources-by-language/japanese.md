## Japanese-language sources on town form

Scope: historic Japanese town form, read from Japanese-language sources, with measured numbers.
Search date: 2026-10-20. Every quote below comes from a page or PDF that was fetched,
unless it is marked "(unverified)". Translations are labelled "(translation: Claude)".
Japanese Wikipedia is labelled "(Japanese Wikipedia)" each time it is used.

### Units used in this report

Old Japanese length units appear throughout. These are the conversion factors used.

| Unit | Meaning | Metres | Source of the factor |
|---|---|---|---|
| 尺 (shaku) | foot | 10/33 m = 0.303 m | Meiji definition, quoted in the Japanese Wikipedia article 丈: 「1尺＝10/33 m（= 約0.303030 m）」 |
| 丈 (jō) | ten shaku | 3.03 m | same article: 丈 = 10 尺 |
| 間 (ken), standard | six shaku; used for land | 1.818 m | same article: 「1間＝6尺 = 約1.8182 m」 |
| 京間 (kyō-ma) ken | Kyoto ken of 6 shaku 5 sun | about 1.97 m | Japanese Wikipedia 京間: 「1間=6尺5寸」; Cabinet Office Meireki fire report: 「１間＝約 1.97 m」 |
| 町 (chō), length | 60 ken | about 109 m | Japanese Wikipedia 町 (単位): 「1町は約109 m」 |
| 町 (chō), area | a land block or area unit | about 1 ha | Cabinet Office Meireki fire report: 「１町は約１ha」 |
| 坪 (tsubo) | one ken squared | about 3.3 m² | derived: 1.818 m × 1.818 m |

Ancient capitals (8th–9th century) used older, slightly different shaku. The sources give
their own metric values for those (for example 40 丈 ≈ 120 m), and this report uses the
source's metres for them, not the Meiji factor.

### Findings

1. **Edo was mostly samurai land.** A Tokyo Metropolitan Government planning booklet,
   drawing on Naitō Akira, gives late-Edo land use as 武家地 (bukechi, samurai land)
   38.7 km² (68%), 寺社地 (jishachi, temple and shrine land) 8.8 km² (16%), and
   町人地 (chōninchi, townspeople's land) 8.9 km² (16%). About 1.3 million people lived
   there. Townspeople's land was extremely dense: over 600 people per hectare.
2. **Samurai land dominates other castle towns too.** Kagoshima (Satsuma domain) was
   described as 「町方三分，武家七分」, 30% town, 70% samurai, with almost 90% of the
   58,000 late-Edo residents of samurai class.
3. **Castle-town blocks were built from a small set of modules.** A 2012 J-STAGE paper
   (Abe and Shinohara) tabulates block form and plot depth for 17 towns founded by
   Hideyoshi and Ieyasu. Plot depth "settled" on 20 ken (kyō-ma) after Osaka's Senba
   district (1598). Square blocks were 40 ken (Osaka Senba), 50 ken (Sunpu, Nagoya) or
   60 ken (Edo). Edo and Nagoya/Sunpu blocks had an empty central courtyard, the
   会所地 (kaishochi, "meeting-place land").
4. **Heian-kyō (Kyoto, 794) had a strict hierarchy.** The basic block (町, machi) was
   40 jō (about 120 m) square. Small streets were 4 jō (about 12 m), major streets
   8 jō (about 24 m) or more, and the central Suzaku avenue 28 jō (about 84 m). The
   smallest house plot, 戸主 (henushi), was 1/32 of a block: 10 jō × 5 jō (about
   460 m²). The planned grid was never filled with houses.
5. **The "narrow frontage because of frontage tax" story is contested.** Popular sources
   (JR West magazine, Kyoto tourism sites) say Hideyoshi taxed by frontage and narrow
   plots resulted. Japanese Wikipedia (with a "citation needed" tag on the folk claim)
   says Edo-period Kyoto taxed each 町 as a whole, split per house (軒役, nokiyaku),
   not by frontage width; frontage-based tax existed in Edo and in post towns, and in
   Kyoto only briefly in early Meiji. A Shogakukan encyclopedia entry confirms that in
   castle towns, duties did move to frontage-based assessment (間口割, maguchi-wari).
6. **The deep narrow plot follows from the block module, not only from tax.** In
   Kyoto's 1590 re-plan (天正地割, Tenshō jiwari), Hideyoshi cut a new north–south lane
   (突抜, tsukinuke) through the middle of each 60-ken square block. A 16th-century town
   record shows a plot 31 ken deep cut to 18 ken, with the back 13 ken handed to the new
   lane. Plots got shallower and more numerous, not deeper.
7. **Fire breaks were placed at specific node types.** After the Meireki fire (1657) Edo
   put 火除地 (hiyokechi, fire-break open land) and 広小路 (hirokōji, widened streets)
   at castle gates, bridgeheads and in front of big temples. Many were later built over
   again; within about 60 years parts reverted to houses (Edo-Tokyo Museum paper).
8. **Post towns were long and thin.** Narai-juku (Nakasendō) ran about 1 km along the
   river; a 1843 survey counted 409 houses, 1 main inn for lords (本陣, honjin), 1
   secondary (脇本陣, waki-honjin), 5 commoner inns (旅籠, hatago). Ishibashi-juku
   (Nikkō road) had a built street of 5 chō 28 ken (about 595 m) with 79 houses and
   30 inns.
9. **Roof orientation follows plot frontage.** In Yakage-juku (Okayama), of 158
   surveyed townhouses, nearly 80% of those under 3 ken frontage had gable-end entrances
   (妻入, tsumairi); over 80% of those 4 ken or wider had eave-side entrances
   (平入, hirairi).

### Case studies

**1. Edo (castle town of the shogun).**
Source: Tokyo Metropolitan Government, 東京の都市づくり, section 04.
「江戸においては、武家地の範囲が大きく、全体面積の約7割を占めていました。」
(translation: Claude) "In Edo, samurai land was extensive and took up about 70% of the
total area."
「江戸は高密な都市であり、特に町人地では、1haあたりの人口が600人を超えていたと考えられ」
(translation: Claude) "Edo was a dense city; in townspeople's land especially, population
is thought to have exceeded 600 per hectare."
Block module, from the Cabinet Office Meireki fire report:
「町割は、一ブロックを京間 60 間（１間＝約 1.97 m）四方とし、中央の 20 間四方の土地を会所地といい、空地とした。町は道路をはさんだ両側町を基本とした。」
(translation: Claude) "The town plan made each block 60 kyō-ma ken square (1 ken ≈ 1.97 m);
the central 20-ken-square plot, called kaishochi, was left empty. A 町 (ward) was basically
both sides of one street." That last point matters: a ward is a street with its two
facing rows (両側町, ryōgawa-machi), not a block.
The same report says outer areas were irregular:
「周辺部では武家屋敷、寺社の割り残り地に不規則な形で町屋が形成された。」
(translation: Claude) "On the periphery, townhouses formed in irregular shapes on land
left over between samurai estates and temples."

**2. Heian-kyō (Kyoto), grid capital.**
Japanese Wikipedia 平安京:
「京内は東西南北に走る大路・小路によって40丈（約120メートル）四方の「町」に分けられていた。」
(translation: Claude) "The city was divided by major and minor streets into 町 blocks
40 jō (about 120 m) square."
「規則正しく配置された条坊が人家で埋まることはついになく、特に右京は平安時代前期から氾濫が続いた」
(translation: Claude) "The regularly laid-out grid was never filled with houses; the right
(western) capital in particular suffered floods from the early Heian period."
Plot subdivision, Nihon Kokugo Daijiten (Kotobank 戸主):
「戸主は東西一〇〇尺、南北五〇尺の区画となり、一三九坪弱（約四六〇平方メートル）の面積となる。」
(translation: Claude) "A henushi is a plot 100 shaku east–west by 50 shaku north–south,
just under 139 tsubo (about 460 m²)."

**3. Heijō-kyō (Nara), plot size by rank.**
Japanese Wikipedia 平城京:
「市街地の宅地は、位階によって大きさが決められ、貴族が占める12町の物を筆頭として、2町・1町・1/2町・1/4町・1/8町・1/16町・1/32町などの宅地が与えられた。」
(translation: Claude) "Urban house plots were sized by court rank: from 12-町 estates held
by nobles down through 2, 1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16 and 1/32 町."
This is a direct rule: plot size is chosen by social rank from a ranked list of block
multiples and fractions. The full list is 12, 2, 1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16 and 1/32 町; the
ranks from 1 町 down are power-of-two fractions of one block, while 12 and 2 町 are
multi-block allocations.

**4. Osaka and the 20-ken plot module.**
Abe and Shinohara 2012, J-STAGE (土木学会論文集D2):
「宅地奥行に関しては，様々な宅地奥行が模索される中で，大坂船場地区以降，奥行京間 20 間に落ち着いていった過程を読み取ることができる．」
(translation: Claude) "As to plot depth, one can read a process in which various depths
were tried and, from Osaka's Senba district onward, depth settled at 20 kyō-ma ken."
The paper also says the textbook rule ("40-ken square blocks and 20-ken plots") is too
simple: where terrain cramped the land between two axis streets, plots shorter than
20 ken but of equal depth filled the gap.

**5. Kyoto's 1590 re-plan and the frontage-tax question.**
For the tax story, JR West "Blue Signal" vol. 111:
「再建される家々には間口の広さに応じて税を課す。京町家の独特の「うなぎの寝床」はこの課税に対抗する生活者の知恵だった。」
(translation: Claude) "Rebuilt houses were taxed according to frontage width. The Kyoto
townhouse's distinctive 'eel's bed' was residents' wisdom against this tax."
Against it, Japanese Wikipedia 京町家:
「しかし実際は、江戸時代の京都の税制は、間口幅に関係なく、まず町に対し総額が賦課され、それを、その町を構成する家持町人が分担する、つまり１軒いくらで負担する制度であった。」
(translation: Claude) "In fact, Edo-period Kyoto's tax system did not depend on frontage
width: a total was levied on the ward, then shared among its house-owning townspeople,
that is, a fixed amount per house."
The same article calls the tax explanation a folk belief (俗説) found in many places
and notes 「明治初頭に新政府が間口長さ3間を１軒役とした時期があった」 (translation:
Claude) "in early Meiji the new government for a time set 3 ken of frontage as one
house-duty." Neither side gives a primary source on the page. My reading: the evidence
fetched here does not settle it; the geometric cause (Finding 6) is better documented.

**6. Kanazawa (Kaga domain), temple belts as defence.**
JR West "Blue Signal" vol. 116:
「3つの台地に寺院群を配して、町は同心円状に広がっている。」
(translation: Claude) "Temple groups were placed on three plateaus, and the town spread
in concentric rings."
「寺町には100を超える寺院を集中させ」 (translation: Claude) "Over 100 temples were
concentrated in Teramachi."
「武家屋敷が城を囲み、町家は主に街道筋に居を構えた。」
(translation: Claude) "Samurai residences surrounded the castle; townhouses stood mainly
along the highway." A Kanazawa City study (vol. 1) adds one measured early plot:
「片岡屋敷は間口十五間、奥行三十三間を測り、街区の東西規模が六十間を測る」
(translation: Claude) "The Kataoka plot measures 15 ken frontage by 33 ken depth, and the
block measures 60 ken east–west."

**7. Kagoshima (Satsuma), an extreme samurai town.**
Kagoshima Prefecture Reimeikan:
「城下は町方三分，武家七分といわれ，武士屋敷の地域が広く，幕末の人口は，五万八千人に達しており，その九割近くを武士階級が占める」
(translation: Claude) "The castle town was said to be three parts town, seven parts
samurai; samurai residential areas were wide; the late-Edo population reached 58,000,
nearly 90% of them samurai class."

**8. Nagoya, a grid castle town with courtyards.**
Nagoya City Library reference report 135, quoting 名古屋城史 p.323: each block left
「中央に二十間四方の空き地を残し、これを会所（かいしょ）と呼んだ」 (translation: Claude)
"a 20-ken-square open space in the centre, called kaisho." Abe and Shinohara's table
gives 10 ken for the same feature. Both are reported; I do not know which is right.
Nagoya City says the 四間道 street was widened to 4 ken (about 7 m) after the 1700 fire,
with back lanes 2–3 m wide.

**9. Post towns (宿場町, shukuba-machi).**
Ishibashi-juku, Heibonsha 日本歴史地名大系 (Kotobank):
「宿村大概帳によれば往還の長さ八町五〇間、宿内町並は南北五町二八間で、宿内人数男一九二・女二二二、家数七九。」
(translation: Claude) "According to the Shukuson Taigaichō survey, the highway section is
8 chō 50 ken long, the built street 5 chō 28 ken north–south, with 192 men, 222 women
and 79 houses." 8 chō 50 ken ≈ 963 m; 5 chō 28 ken ≈ 595 m.
Yakage-juku, Nara National Research Institute bulletin 2018:
「間口別にみると、３間未満では80％近くが妻入であり、反対に４間以上では80％以上が平入であった。」
(translation: Claude) "By frontage, nearly 80% of houses under 3 ken were gable-entry;
conversely, over 80% of those 4 ken or wider were eave-entry."
The same bulletin notes the oldest map (1689) records each plot's 表間口 (street
frontage), because post-horse duty and ground-rent exemption were tied to it.

### What this adds to the English-language research

- **Measured class shares.** Edo 68 / 16 / 16 (samurai / temple / town) and Kagoshima
  70 / 30 give real numbers for a three-class zoning that English summaries usually
  state only as "about 70%".
- **Density by class.** About 670 people/ha in townspeople's land versus about 170 in
  samurai land: a four-fold contrast inside one city. European walled towns in the
  earlier research do not split density by class this way.
- **A module catalogue.** Abe and Shinohara give block and plot dimensions for 17 towns
  in one table. The "40-ken block, 20-ken plot" rule is a convergence over 25 years,
  not a single standard.
- **Ward = street, not block.** The 両側町 idea (a ward owns both sides of a street) is a
  different topology from European "block = parish quarter".
- **Courtyard blocks.** The empty central 会所地 in Edo and Nagoya blocks is a
  distinctive feature; it later filled with back-alley tenements.
- **Grid capitals were never full.** Heian-kyō's western half emptied early; a planned
  grid and the built area diverge.
- **The frontage-tax story is weaker than English sources often imply.** Japanese
  Wikipedia flags it as folk belief for Kyoto; geometric subdivision is better attested.

### What this means for a generator and rule packs

- **Class zoning as data.** A Japanese castle-town rule pack sets three land classes with
  target area shares (for example samurai 0.68, temple 0.16, town 0.16 for an Edo-like
  capital; 0.70 / 0.30 combined for Kagoshima) and per-class density targets
  (town about 670/ha, samurai about 170/ha, temple about 57/ha).
- **Block module as data.** Block side (40, 50 or 60 ken), optional central courtyard
  (none, 10 or 20 ken), plot depth (20 ken; 15 ken on corners) are pack parameters,
  in ken, with a ken length (1.818 m or 1.97 m) chosen per pack.
- **Ward topology.** Town wards are generated as street segments with both frontages
  (両側町), so ward labels sit on streets, not inside blocks.
- **Plot-size-by-rank rule.** For grid capitals, house plots are chosen by rank from the
  full Heijō-kyō list: 12, 2, 1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16 and 1/32 町 (one 町 = one block;
  12 and 2 町 are multi-block allocations, the rest power-of-two fractions of one block);
  this is a closed, compilable rule.
- **Node-anchored open spaces.** Fire-break squares are placed at castle gates,
  bridgeheads and temple approaches, not at random; temple belts sit on the town edge or
  on high ground.
- **Renderer hint.** Townhouse roof orientation is a function of frontage: under 3 ken
  draw gable-to-street, 4 ken and over draw eave-to-street.
