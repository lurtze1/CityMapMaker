## French, Portuguese and Arabic sources on African town form

Scope: historic sub-Saharan towns, read through French, Portuguese, Arabic, Dutch and Italian
primary sources, plus African-published or African-state-authored scholarship. Every claim has a
fetched URL. Claims seen only in search-result snippets are marked "(unverified)". Unit
conversions and derived densities are my own arithmetic and are labelled "(derived)".

Terms used below:
- **banco**: sun-dried mud brick and mud plaster (West African French usage).
- **concession**: a walled family compound (French usage in West Africa); the plot unit.
- **toguere**: a raised mound on which Djenné houses sit above the flood.
- **jugol**: the wall of Harar, and by extension the walled old town.
- **iya**: Edo name for the Benin earthwork ditches-and-banks.
- **afin**: Yoruba palace compound.
- **mitaa**: Swahili wards (singular *mtaa*).
- **kebele**: modern Ethiopian neighbourhood administrative unit.
- **ICOMOS evaluation**: the expert report that precedes a UNESCO listing; usually has numbers the
  short UNESCO page leaves out.
- OCR: machine-read text of a scanned book. Old French and Portuguese OCR prints the long "s" as
  "f" ("maifons" = "maisons"). I quote the OCR as printed and give the reading.

### Findings

1. **Walls with counted gates are the norm in the Sahel, and the chroniclers count them.** The
   Tarikh al-Sudan (c. 1655) says Djenné had a wall with 11 gates, 3 later blocked, leaving 8.
   Caillié (1828) measured Djenné's mud wall as 10 feet high and 14 inches thick, "two and a half
   milles" round. Harar had 5 historic gates, each giving onto its own quarter; a sixth was added
   in the 19th century. Gondar's royal enclosure has a 900 m wall with 12 gates.
2. **Not every Sudanic town was walled.** Leo Africanus (French ed. 1556) calls Gao "a very great
   city ... without walls". Caillié says Timbuktu "is closed by no enclosure; one can enter from
   all sides". The Tarikh says early Timbuktu had "very low walls, so that from outside one saw
   what happened inside". Agadez and Kano, by contrast, are described as walled.
3. **Mosque + palace + market form a fixed civic core.** In Djenné the sultan turned his palace
   into the Great Mosque and built a new palace "adjoining the mosque on the east side" (Tarikh).
   Today the mosque fronts a 400 m by 150 m market esplanade that splits the town in two halves
   (Ducoloner, Bamako 1999). In Agadez the Sultan's palace (1.2 ha) sits beside the Great Mosque
   enclosure (5,600 m²). In Harar the main mosque, central market and main square form "the three
   points of a triangle" (ICOMOS).
4. **Quarters follow social origin.** Agadez has 11 irregular quarters that preserve the outlines
   of the Tuareg camps the town grew from, named by group or trade (potters, servants, caravan
   square, outer market). Djenné has 10 quarters, 7 east of the esplanade. The Tarikh records that
   Moroccan invaders in 1591 chose "the most flourishing quarter, that of the Ghadamès merchants"
   for their citadel (kasbah).
5. **House type: one- or two-storey mud courtyard houses with blank street walls.** Caillié: houses
   in Djenné have one upper storey, flat roofs, no outside windows, and rooms lit only from an
   inner courtyard; Timbuktu houses are ground-floor only. Agadez houses are 1 or 2 levels and
   3 to 6 m high. Poor and enslaved people lived in round straw huts inside and outside Timbuktu.
6. **Plot sizes in Djenné are small and irregular.** A dense-quarter compound of about 100 m²
   "fits into the neighbouring compounds" with "a completely dislocated shape". Small squares,
   Koranic schools and cemeteries recur every 50 to 100 m along a street.
7. **Swahili coast: stone towns in Portuguese eyes, wood in Ibn Battuta's.** Barbosa (c. 1516)
   describes Kilwa, Mombasa and Malindi as towns of "houses of stone and lime" with terraces and
   carved doors, Malindi "very well laid out in streets". Ibn Battuta (1331) says Kilwa is "all of
   wood" with reed roofs. The two are 185 years apart; I do not know which describes the
   majority of houses at either date. Gedi reached 34 ha in the 14th century and later had at
   least 300 stone houses (French IFAO monograph).
8. **Benin City had a planned grid of very wide streets.** Dapper (Dutch, 1668): 30 very straight
   broad streets, each about 120 feet wide, with narrower cross streets; houses in rows, one
   storey, red clay walls polished "like a mirror". Pacheco Pereira (Portuguese, c. 1505): the
   city is about one league long "from gate to gate", has no wall but "a great ditch, very wide
   and deep", and houses of rammed earth (taipa) roofed with palm.
9. **Very large, low-density capitals.** Old Oyo: three wall circuits of 7.5, 18 and 28 km; the
   inner one encloses the palace in 200 ha; the outer two enclose nearly 3,000 ha, a third
   covered by mud-building ruins. Mbanza Kongo: a plateau "perhaps 10 miles round" holding "more
   than 100,000 people", with walled Portuguese and royal precincts each about one mile round
   (Pigafetta/Lopes 1591).
10. **Measured densities from an African-published book.** McIntosh (in a 1999 Bamako book)
    uses 221 people/ha for modern Djenné, 237/ha for nearby rural settlements, and 389/ha for
    traditional towns of the inland Niger delta.

### Case studies

**1. Djenné (Mali), Arabic chronicle plus French field survey.**
Tarikh al-Sudan, Arabic (Houdas ed., 1898), OCR as printed:
> "وهى حبطة بالسور ولها احدى عثمر باب ثم سدوا الثلاثة فتى على ثمانية ابواب"

The OCR has errors; it reads "وهي محيطة بالسور ولها إحدى عشر باب ثم سدوا الثلاثة فبقي على ثمانية
أبواب". French translation (Houdas 1900):
> "Dienné est entourée d'un rempart qui était percé de onze portes. Trois d'entre elles ont été
> bouchées par la suite, en sorte qu'il n'en reste plus aujourd'hui que huit."

(translation: Claude) "Djenné is surrounded by a rampart that had eleven gates. Three were
later blocked, so only eight remain today."

Same source on the civic core:
> "le sultan démolit son palais et le remplaça par un temple ... c'est la grande mosquée actuelle.
> Il construisit un autre palais ... et ce palais avoisine la mosquée du côté est."

(translation: Claude) "The sultan demolished his palace and replaced it with a temple; it is
today's Great Mosque. He built another palace, which adjoins the mosque on the east side."

Ducoloner, in *Djenné d'hier à demain* (Bamako, Éditions Donniya, 1999):
> "Cette esplanade de 400 mètres de long par 150 mètres de large, orientée sud-nord, constitue
> l'axe central de la ville et la partage en deux zones sensiblement égales"

(translation: Claude) "This esplanade, 400 m long by 150 m wide and running south to north, is
the town's central axis and splits it into two roughly equal zones."
> "La concession de Monsieur Bah a une surface foncière très petite, de l'ordre de 100 m2 ... et
> une forme complètement disloquée qui s'imbrique dans les concessions voisines."

(translation: Claude) "Mr Bah's compound has a very small plot, about 100 m², and a completely
dislocated shape that interlocks with the neighbouring compounds."

Caillié (1830) on houses:
> "La plupart ont un étage ... elles sont toutes à terrasse, n'ont pas de fenêtres à l'extérieur,
> et les chambres ne reçoivent d'air que par une cour intérieure."

(translation: Claude) "Most have one upper storey; all have flat roofs, no outside windows, and
the rooms get air only from an inner courtyard."

**2. Timbuktu (Mali): growth from thorn enclosures to a continuous fabric.**
Tarikh al-Sudan, French tr. (Houdas 1900):
> "Au début, les demeures des habitants consistaient en enclos d'épines, en paillottes; puis elles
> se transformèrent en huttes d'argile. Enfin la ville fut entourée de murs très bas"

(translation: Claude) "At first the dwellings were thorn enclosures and straw huts; then they
became clay huts. Finally the town was surrounded by very low walls."
> "l'enchevêtrement des maisons et la continuité des constructions ne s'acheva que dans le milieu
> du xe siècle [de l'hégire]"

(translation: Claude) "The interlocking of houses and the continuity of building were only
completed in the middle of the tenth [Islamic] century" (that is, the mid-1500s AD).

Caillié (1830):
> "La ville de Temboctou peut avoir trois milles de tour; elle forme une espèce de triangle"
> "Les rues de Temboctou sont propres et assez larges pour y passer trois cavaliers de front"

(translation: Claude) "Timbuktu may be three miles round; it forms a kind of triangle." "The
streets are clean and wide enough for three horsemen abreast."

Leo Africanus, French ed. 1556 (OCR as printed): "Les maifons d'icelle font detorrss platres, &
conuertes de paille. Il y a ... vn temple de pierre, & chaus". Reading: "Les maisons d'icelle
sont de torchis plâtrés, et couvertes de paille. Il y a un temple de pierre et chaux."
(translation: Claude) "Its houses are of plastered wattle-and-daub, roofed with straw. There is
a temple [mosque] of stone and lime."

**3. Agadez (Niger): quarters as fossil camps.** ICOMOS evaluation (French):
> "Elle comprend 11 quartiers aux formes irrégulières, témoins des limites des anciens campements
> des tribus touarègues au moment où elles ont commencé à se sédentariser."

(translation: Claude) "It has 11 irregularly shaped quarters, which record the limits of the old
Tuareg tribal camps when they began to settle."
> "un tissu urbain aux rues plus larges et aux nombreuses placettes"

(translation: Claude) "an urban fabric with wider streets and many small squares" (contrasted in
the same text with the hierarchical street system of the "traditional Arab-Islamic city").

**4. Harar (Ethiopia): gate = quarter.** ICOMOS evaluation (French):
> "Les cinq portes historiques correspondaient aux routes principales vers la ville. À l'origine,
> cette dernière était elle-même divisée en cinq quartiers correspondant aux portes"

(translation: Claude) "The five historic gates matched the main roads into the town. Originally
the town itself was divided into five quarters matching the gates."
> "Les principales fonctions publiques sont concentrées dans la zone publique centrale, qui forme
> un triangle."

(translation: Claude) "The main public functions are concentrated in the central public zone,
which forms a triangle" (horse market square, central market, main mosque).

**5. Kilwa, Mombasa, Malindi, Mogadishu (Swahili coast): Portuguese and Arabic eyes.**
Duarte Barbosa, *Livro* (c. 1516; 1946 Lisbon ed.):
> "uma vila de mouros de mui formosas casas de pedra e cal, com muitas janelas à nossa maneira
> ... com muitos terrados, as portas de madeira mui bem lavradas"

(translation: Claude) "a Muslim town of very fine houses of stone and lime, with many windows in
our manner, many flat roof-terraces, and very well carved wooden doors" (Kilwa).
> "uma cidade de que chamam Mombaça, a qual é muito formosa, de mui altas casas de pedra e cal"

(translation: Claude) "a city called Mombasa, very handsome, with very tall houses of stone and
lime."
> "O qual lugar é de mui formosas casas de pedra e cal, de muitos sobrados ... o lugar está mui
> bem arruado."

(translation: Claude) "The place has very fine stone-and-lime houses of many storeys; the place
is very well laid out in streets" (Malindi).

Anonymous pilot's account of Cabral's voyage (1500; Portuguese 1812 retranslation from Italian):
> "as suas casas são altas ao modo de Hespanha"

(translation: Claude) "its houses are tall, in the manner of Spain" (Kilwa).

Ibn Battuta, *Tuhfat al-nuzzar* (1331 visit), Arabic:
> "ومدينة كلوا من أحسن المدن وأتقنها عمارة وكلها بالخشب. وسقف بيوتها الديس"

(translation: Claude) "The city of Kilwa is among the finest and best-built of cities, and all of
it is of wood. The roofs of its houses are of *dīs* reed."
On Mogadishu: "وهي مدينة متناهية في الكبر" (translation: Claude) "it is a city of the utmost size."

**6. Benin City (Nigeria): Portuguese and Dutch descriptions.**
Duarte Pacheco Pereira, *Esmeraldo de situ orbis* (c. 1505; OCR as printed):
> "efta Qdade terá huma leguoa de comprido de porta a porta & nom tem muro foomente hc cercada
> de huma grande caua muito largua & fiinda ... & tem as cafas de taypa cobertas de palma"

Reading: "esta cidade terá uma légua de comprido de porta a porta e não tem muro, somente é
cercada de uma grande cava muito larga e funda ... e tem as casas de taipa cobertas de palma."
(translation: Claude) "This city is about a league long from gate to gate and has no wall; it is
only surrounded by a great ditch, very wide and deep. Its houses are of rammed earth roofed with
palm."

Olfert Dapper, *Naukeurige beschrijvinge der Afrikaensche gewesten* (1668; OCR, long s
normalised):
> "De stadt heeft dertigh zeer rechte en brede straten, ieder ontrent van hondert en twintigh
> voeten breet ... daer over al veel breede dwars-straten, doch wat smalder, op uitkomen."

(translation: Claude) "The city has thirty very straight and broad streets, each about a hundred
and twenty feet wide, into which run many broad cross streets, though somewhat narrower."
> "De huizen staen er langs henen in goede orde gebouwt, dicht by een ... zijn niet hoger dan een
> stadie"

(translation: Claude) "The houses stand along them in good order, close together; they are no
higher than one storey."

Caution: Dapper never went to Africa and compiled from Dutch company reports (search snippet,
unverified); his plates are thought to be partly invented by the publisher (unverified).

**7. Mbanza Kongo / São Salvador (Angola).** Filippo Pigafetta from Duarte Lopes, *Relatione
del Reame di Congo* (Rome 1591; Italian, from a Portuguese informant; OCR as printed):
> "haue vn piano tutto coltiuato, & fornito di cafali, & villate, circondante forfe 1o. miglia,
> oue fi alloggiano ... più di 100. milia perfone."

(translation: Claude) "[The mountain] has on top a plain, all cultivated and furnished with
hamlets and villages, perhaps ten miles round, where more than 100,000 people live."
> "Il circuito de Portoghefi abbraccia d'intorno ad vn miglio, & altre tanto li cafamenti reali"

(translation: Claude) "The Portuguese precinct is about one mile round, and the royal buildings
the same." Between the two walled precincts is "a large space" with the main church and its
square. Outside, lords build wherever suits them, so "the size of this city cannot be
determined".

Portuguese secondary source (Boletim D. António Barroso, no. 22):
> "Em 1630 fizeram-se aqui cerca de 5 mil baptismos, numa população de 100 mil habitantes."

(translation: Claude) "In 1630 about 5,000 baptisms were held here, in a population of 100,000."
It also gives "cerca de 600 pessoas" (about 600 people) living there in 1881. No primary source is
cited for the 1630 figure.

**8. Old Oyo (Nigeria): concentric walls at metropolitan scale.** UNESCO tentative list entry,
submitted by Nigeria's National Commission for Museums and Monuments:
> "The old palace compound and a past water reservoir lie within the 200 ha area enclosed by a
> 7.5 km long wall. Two concentric outer walls with ditches, 18 and 28 km long, enclose nearly
> 3,000 ha: one third of this is covered by the ruins of past mud buildings and one third by
> sherd scatters presumed to have occurred under more ephemeral housing."

**9. Gondar (Ethiopia): a walled palace city.** UNESCO (French):
> "entourée d'une muraille de 900 m percée de douze portes et dotée de trois ponts"

(translation: Claude) "surrounded by a 900 m wall pierced by twelve gates and provided with three
bridges" (Fasil Ghebbi, royal enclosure, from 1636).

**10. Kano and Agadez in Leo Africanus (French 1556).** Kano: "vne cité ... enuironnée de murs
de craye, comme les maifons même" (reading: "environnée de murs de craie, comme les maisons
mêmes"). (translation: Claude) "a city surrounded by walls of chalk [likely meaning pale earth],
as are the houses themselves." Agadez: "eft vne cité ceinte de murailles ... Les maifons font fort
bien bâties, en la maniere de celles de Barbarie". (translation: Claude) "is a walled city; the
houses are very well built, in the manner of those of North Africa."

**11. Gedi (Kenya): growth by stages.** IFAO review of Pradines 2010 (French):
> "elle s'accroît autour d'une mosquée au xiie siècle, double sa superficie au début du xiiie
> siècle et atteint son extension maximale (34 ha) au xive siècle"
> "la ville se déplace du nord-est au sud-ouest avec la création d'une nouvelle mosquée du
> vendredi et de 300 maisons en pierre au minimum, tandis que l'aire de la première grande
> mosquée se retrouve hors les murs."

(translation: Claude) "It grows around a mosque in the 12th century, doubles its area in the
early 13th, and reaches its maximum extent (34 ha) in the 14th." "The town shifts from north-east
to south-west, with a new Friday mosque and at least 300 stone houses, while the area of the
first great mosque ends up outside the walls."

### What this adds to the English-language research

- **Counted gates and their history.** The Tarikh gives the exact Djenné gate count and the fact
  that gates were blocked over time. That is a rule a generator can use: a wall's gate count can
  shrink, and blocked gates leave road stubs.
- **A developmental sequence for one town.** The Tarikh gives Timbuktu's stages: thorn
  enclosures, straw huts, clay huts, low wall, mosques, then a continuous built fabric about a
  century later. English summaries usually skip this.
- **Plot and open-space spacing in a living Sahelian town.** The Bamako 1999 book gives a 100 m²
  dense-quarter compound, 200 m² neighbourhood cemeteries, small squares every 50–100 m, and the
  400 × 150 m central esplanade.
- **Density constants from African-published work.** 221, 237 and 389 people/ha (Djenné and the
  delta). These are the only density constants I found that are tied to a named Sahelian place.
- **Quarter genesis.** French ICOMOS texts state that Agadez quarters are former camp outlines
  and that Harar quarters were defined by gates. These are two different rules for drawing wards.
- **Benin street widths.** Dapper's "30 streets, about 120 feet wide" in the Dutch original is a
  measured (if second-hand) number. Pacheco Pereira's earlier "ditch, no wall" corrects the idea
  that Benin had a masonry wall.
- **Contradictions are visible only in the originals.** Ibn Battuta's "all of wood" Kilwa versus
  Barbosa's "stone and lime" Kilwa; Caillié's 10–12 thousand versus UNESCO's 100,000 for
  Timbuktu. A spec must pick an era; the sources do not average.

### What this means for a generator and rule packs

- **Add a "Sudanic earthen" rule pack.** Civic core = mosque + palace adjacent on one side +
  market esplanade (Djenné: palace east of mosque; esplanade about 400 × 150 m). Houses are
  courtyard compounds of 1 to 2 storeys with blank street walls, plots from about 100 m² upward.
  Insert a small square, school or cemetery every 50–100 m of street. Density range 220–390
  people/ha. Source: Tarikh al-Sudan, Caillié, Bamako 1999.
- **Make "walled" a per-town choice, not a culture default.** The sources show walled (Djenné,
  Kano, Agadez, Harar), low-walled (early Timbuktu), ditch-only (Benin c. 1505), and unwalled
  (Gao, Timbuktu 1828) towns in the same region. A gate count with "blocked" gates is a useful
  extra parameter.
- **Support two ward-generation rules.** (a) Gate-defined wards: one ward per gate sector
  (Harar). (b) Seed-camp wards: irregular wards grown from several separate seeds, each labelled by
  group or trade (Agadez). Both are data, not code (closed constraint kinds per ADR-017).
- **Add a "Swahili stone town" pack.** Dense stone-and-lime houses with roof terraces, narrow
  streets, a seafront row, small mitaa wards, several mosques; town area 16–34 ha (Lamu, Gedi).
  Allow mixed stone and thatch fabric, since the sources disagree on the share.
- **Add a "Yoruba/Edo metropolitan" pack with concentric walls.** Inner palace wall enclosing a
  very large palace precinct (Old Oyo: 200 ha), and outer wall circuits enclosing thousands of
  hectares at low density (about 20–50 people/ha, derived), much of it farmland and sparse
  housing. Benin variant: wide straight streets (about 34 m, derived from Dapper) and a ditch-bank
  instead of a masonry wall.
- **Record the source era in the spec.** The numbers for one city differ by a factor of ten
  between eras (Timbuktu 100,000 in the 16th c. vs 10–12 thousand in 1828; Mbanza Kongo 100,000
  in 1630 vs about 600 in 1881).
