## Chinese-language sources on town form

Scope: historic Chinese walled towns, mainly Ming (1368-1644) and Qing (1644-1911) county and prefecture seats, plus the classical ideal (Kaogong ji) and the Tang-Song street change. Sources are Chinese primary texts (classics, gazetteers, the Qing encyclopedia *Gujin Tushu Jicheng*), Chinese-language scholarship, and two English-language papers built on Chinese gazetteer data.

Jargon used below:
- **County seat (县城 xiancheng)**: the walled town where a county magistrate sat. The lowest rank of walled administrative town.
- **Prefecture (府 fu)** and **department (州 zhou)**: ranks above or beside the county. A prefectural city usually also held one or two counties "attached to the wall" (附郭 fuguo), which had no wall of their own.
- **Yamen (衙署)**: a government office compound.
- **Gazetteer (地方志 difangzhi)**: an official local history. Its "walls and moats" section (城池) records wall length, height, gates and moat.
- **li (里)**: a length unit, about half a kilometre (exact factor below). Not to be confused with *li* the household group.
- **bu (步)**, **zhang (丈)**, **chi (尺)**: pace, ten-foot rod, foot.

Unit conversion used in this report (one factor, applied everywhere unless a source gives its own):
- Qing official builder's foot (营造尺) = 32 cm; 1 li = 180 zhang = 360 bu = 1,800 chi = 576 m. Source: Chinese Wikipedia, 中國度量衡, row "營造尺庫平制：1里＝18引＝180丈＝360步＝1800尺…＝576米". https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/中國度量衡
- The Skinner research group (used by Ioannides and Zhang) instead used 1 li = 0.5 km and 1 zhang = 3.33 m. https://wordpress.clarku.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/423/2016/03/WalledCitiesJUEFinal.pdf
- So a wall given in li has an uncertainty of about 15% from the unit alone (500 vs 576 m per li). I show both where it matters.
- Song-Yuan foot = 31.2 cm (same Wikipedia table, row 宋元 "1尺＝31.2"). A Song-Yuan bu of 5 chi is then 1.56 m.

### Findings

1. **Wall size follows administrative rank, and the classical commentary already said so.** Jia Gongyan's Tang sub-commentary on the Kaogong ji, citing Zheng Xuan, gives walls by noble rank: "公之城蓋方九里，侯伯七里，子男五里" (translation: Claude: "a duke's city is perhaps nine li square, a marquis's or earl's seven li, a viscount's or baron's five li"), and reckons the Son of Heaven at twelve li. https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/周禮註疏/卷四十一
   Real Ming-Qing walls show the same ladder. My count of 1,252 wall entries in the Qing encyclopedia gives median circuits of 9.0 li (prefecture), 6.0 li (department) and 4.3 li (county). Ioannides and Zhang, from Gu Zuyu's late-Ming geography, get means of 10.2, 6.65 and 4.44 li. The two independent sources agree closely.

2. **The usual county seat had four gates, a moat, and a wall of 2-3.5 km.** In the Qing encyclopedia, 59% of county entries that state a gate count say four (207 of 351). The Skinner data for the 19th century give a mean of 4.24 gates, 96% with a moat, mean wall height 7.47 m (Ioannides and Zhang, Table 1). County quartiles: 3.0 li to 6.0 li, i.e. 1.7-3.5 km at 576 m/li.

3. **Not every seat was walled, and walls usually fitted the built area.** Xue et al. (with Cheng Yinong's wall compilation) find that in 1368 only 851 of 1,375 cities (62%) had walls, rising to 95% by 1576. They state "city walls in this period were usually slightly larger than the built-up area of the city." https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/13/5071/2021/essd-13-5071-2021.html
   The Qing encyclopedia has 78 entries with the phrase "舊無城/向無城" ("formerly no wall"). Taiwan's three seats were still "城池未建" ("wall not yet built") when it was compiled.

4. **Terrain beat the ideal.** The oldest Chinese siting rule, in the *Guanzi*, says the opposite of the Kaogong ji grid: "因天材，就地利，故城郭不必中規矩，道路不必中準繩" (translation: Claude: "use what nature provides, follow the advantage of the ground; so walls need not match compass and square, and roads need not match the plumb line"). https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/管子/第05篇乘馬
   Kangxi-era official prefecture maps record a city's facing direction. In Qujing prefecture four of the seven walled towns are noted "城向東北" or "城向東南" because of mountains and rivers. Only those on wide flat basins are "城向南" (faces south) (Li Xiaocong 2023).

5. **Temples and offices: fixed set, variable position.** Secondary evidence only (the decree is quoted from a modern repost; the primary text was not fetched, see Not found): by decree of 1370 every prefecture, department and county City God temple was to be built "依各該地公廨起盖" (translation: Claude: "on the model of that place's government office"). City Gods were ranked like officials: prefecture 威靈公 (duke), department 靈佑侯 (marquis), county 顯佑伯 (earl). (Zhang Chuanyong, repost on 163.com.) Position inside the town was not fixed. In Fengshan county (Taiwan) the Confucian temple and City God temple stood outside the north gate during the earth-wall phase.

6. **Ward system to street system.** Tang Chang'an's 110 walled wards measured 500-838 m N-S by 550-1,125 m E-W. Each had walls and two or four gates, with internal streets about 15 m wide (Chinese Wikipedia, 唐长安城). Japanese scholarship, summarised in Chinese by Liang Jianguo, dates the full collapse of the ward walls to the end of Northern Song at the latest (Kato Shigeru). Umehara Ikuo puts it earlier, at early Song or the Five Dynasties, driven by "侵街" (street encroachment). After the Xining era (1068-77) people located places by street and lane names, not ward names.

7. **Lane widths and lot size in Yuan Dadu (Beijing).** The *Xijin zhi*: "大街二十四步阔，小街十二步阔" (translation: Claude: "main streets 24 bu wide, minor streets 12 bu wide"). Wang Bin's reading of the 1965 boring survey: fire lanes 6 bu ≈ 9.4 m, hutong 3 bu ≈ 4-5 m, with Zhuanta hutong measured at 4 m average. The *Yuanshi* fixes a residential grant of eight mu: "仍定制以地八畝為一分" (translation: Claude: "and fixed the rule that eight mu of land make one share"). https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/元史/卷013

8. **Density: Song capitals were very dense.** Bao Weimin's estimates, as reported in a Chinese repost: Bianjing (Kaifeng), late Northern Song, 12,000-13,000 people/km². Lin'an (Hangzhou), Chunyou era (1241-52), about 21,000, perhaps 35,000 in the Xianchun era (1265-74). I found no measured density for an ordinary Ming-Qing county seat.

Caveats on my parse of the Qing encyclopedia: it takes the first circuit stated in each entry, which can be an older wall before an extension. Entries that say "有奇" (and a bit more) are taken at the round number. Entries in zhang are divided by 180, in bu by 360. 267 of 1,519 entries gave no parsable circuit. Gate counts are parsed only where the text says "門N" (gates: N). Entries that only list gate names are missed.

### Case studies

1. **Fengshan county old town (Zuoying, Kaohsiung), Taiwan.** Earth wall 1722, stone wall 1826: "城牆周圍 864丈（約2,765公尺）…城門、砲臺各4座" (translation: Claude: "wall circuit 864 zhang, about 2,765 m … four gates and four gun platforms"). Before the stone wall, "北門外有文廟、城隍廟" (translation: Claude: "outside the north gate stood the Confucian temple and the City God temple"). The yamen and the garrison commander's office sat on the highest ground and faced south-east, following the slope. The county yamen compound was about 175 × 70 m and was the largest office. Source: Liao Dezong 2012.
2. **Qujing prefecture, Yunnan (Kangxi-era official map).** The towns stand in river basins. The map notes each town's facing: Malong "城向東北", Xundian "城向東南", Zhanyi (described as "背山面河", back to the mountain, facing the river) "城向東北", Luoping "城向東北". Only Qujing, Luliang and Pingyi on wide basins "城向南". Source: Li Xiaocong 2023.
3. **Wangdu and Daming counties, Hebei (surveyed maps).** At Wangdu, river erosion pulled in the north-east wall: "本應方形的城垣，因受九龍河侧蚀，使得東北城墻内縮" (translation: Claude: "a wall that should have been square had its north-east side pulled in by erosion from the Jiulong river"). Suburbs (关厢 guanxiang, built-up strips outside a gate) grew only at the north and south gates, on the main road. At Daming, the Wei river landing drew large suburbs to the south and east gates and few to the north and west. Source: Li Xiaocong 2023.
4. **Huizhou prefecture and She county, Anhui.** The prefectural wall is 9 li 70 bu with five gates. The moat runs on three sides only: "南及東、南以山險無濠" (translation: Claude: "on the south and south-east there is no moat, because the hills are steep"). She county had no wall until 1554, then built one of 7 li: "西南以府城為屏庇，餘三面皆山，不池而險" (translation: Claude: "on the south-west it uses the prefectural wall as a screen; the other three sides are all hills, so it is strong without a moat"). Source: Gujin Tushu Jicheng juan 20.
5. **Langzhong, Sichuan (feng shui exemplar).** Gu Zuyu: the county is named "以閬水紆曲繞縣三面" (translation: Claude: "because the Lang river winds around the county on three sides"). The old earth wall was "在嘉陵江北，對峙錦屏山" (translation: Claude: "north of the Jialing river, facing Jinping mountain across it"). It was rebuilt in stone in 1371: "為門四，周九里有奇" (translation: Claude: "four gates, a circuit of a little over nine li"). Modern Chinese scholarship (Wu Qifu) treats Langzhong as the model feng-shui city from Tang-Song on. Source: Dushi fangyu jiyao juan 68; Wu Qifu, UESTC journal.
6. **Suzhou prefecture.** Original wall "大城周四十二里三十步" with eight gates. "宋初，塞二門" (translation: Claude: "in early Song two gates were blocked"), leaving six. Ming-Qing: "城為亞字形" (the wall is shaped like the character 亞, a cross with notched corners). It is 4,483 zhang 6 chi 5 cun long, with six land gates and five water gates. Source: Gujin Tushu Jicheng juan 20.
7. **Pingyao county, Shanxi.** Rebuilt 1370: "周九里十三步，高三丈…門六" (translation: Claude: "circuit 9 li 13 bu, height 3 zhang … six gates"), with 94 bastions. It is a county seat with six gates and a circuit near the prefectural median. Source: Gujin Tushu Jicheng juan 18.
8. **Songjiang prefecture.** "周圍九里一百七十三步…門四…濠深七尺，闊十丈" (translation: Claude: "circuit 9 li 173 bu … four gates … moat 7 chi deep, 10 zhang wide"). A separate walled granary town of 2 li lay outside the west gate. Shanghai county, in the same prefecture, was walled only in the 1550s against pirates: 9 li. Source: Gujin Tushu Jicheng juan 20. Huang Jingbin's study of Songjiang is listed under Not found.
9. **Tang Chang'an (ideal grid realised).** The outer wall is 9,721 × 8,652 m with 110 walled wards and two markets of about 1 km² each. Zhuque avenue is 150-155 m wide. Source: zh.wikipedia 唐长安城. The ward walls and night curfew are what Song cities lost.
10. **Yuan Dadu (Beijing).** It is the planned-grid capital with a measured street hierarchy: 24/12/6/3 bu (≈36/18/9.4/4.5 m). The Xisi-bei hutongs 1-8 run east-west, 600 m (first lane) down to 424 m (eighth lane) long. Source: Wang Bin.

### What this adds to the English-language research

Gaps filled:
- **A rank-to-size table from 1,250 gazetteer walls.** This is new to the corpus. Prefecture, department and county have distinct medians: about 5.2, 3.5 and 2.5 km. It matches Ioannides and Zhang (late Ming), who have an English paper but which the corpus did not cite.
- **Gate counts as a distribution, not a rule.** Four gates is the mode for county seats (59%), but three, five and six are common. Prefectural cities spread wider.
- **Unit factors.** 1 li = 576 m (Qing official foot), against 500 m in the Skinner data.
- **Classical text with commentary.** The Kaogong ji passage and Zheng Xuan's notes are now quoted from the original. The rank-scaled wall sizes (9/7/5 li) come from the commentary, not the Kaogong ji itself.
- **Terrain overrides orientation.** The Kangxi maps record "城向東北" and similar, so south-facing is a default with exceptions. The Guanzi says so explicitly.

Claims confirmed:
- Suzhou wall perimeter. The corpus says about 4.5 × 3 km, a perimeter of about 15 km. The gazetteer gives 4,483 zhang ≈ 14.3 km (at 3.2 m) or 14.9 km (at 3.33 m).
- Suburbs outside gates along roads and water (corpus: Suzhou, Guangzhou). Li Xiaocong's Wangdu, Daming and Jining examples confirm the pattern for ordinary county seats.
- Hangzhou's very high density. Bao Weimin's 21,000-35,000/km² supports the corpus's "1,000,000+" order of magnitude.

Claims contradicted or needing correction:
- **Suzhou gates.** The corpus lists five gates (Pan, Xu, Chang, Qi, Lou) from the 1229 stele. The Qing gazetteer text says six land gates after early Song: "惟閶、胥、盤、葑、婁、齊六門". It adds the east gate Feng (葑), plus five water gates. Either the corpus misread the stele or Feng gate was closed in 1229. This needs checking against the stele.
- **Pingyao.** The corpus says no plan was found. The gazetteer text at least gives its wall (9 li 13 bu, 6 gates, 94 bastions), so a spec can be written without a plan.
- **"Chinese city = Kaogong ji grid" (if any English source implies it).** Cheng Yinong argues that layout similarity does not prove the Kaogong ji was followed. He also argues that city-form history is not a linear development: "至少目前的研究無法證明這種'發展史'的存在" (translation: Claude: "at least current research cannot prove that such a 'developmental history' exists").

### What this means for a generator and rule packs

- **Size from rank, as data.** A Chinese rule pack takes a seat rank (prefecture / department / county / garrison) and draws the wall circuit from the measured quartiles: county 1.7-3.5 km, department 2.2-5.2 km, prefecture 3.4-7.1 km. It does not use a fixed size. The Kaogong ji 9/7/5 ladder becomes an optional "ideal" variant chosen by the spec author.
- **Gate count as a weighted choice.** For a county seat the weights are about 4: 0.59, 3: 0.16, 5: 0.12, 6+: 0.09, 1-2: 0.04. Gates are placed on sides facing roads or water. A side against a steep hill may have no gate and no moat (Huizhou).
- **Orientation is an input with a terrain override.** The default axis faces south. When a spec gives mountains or a river, the axis may face the open side. Record "faces" as a resolved, pinnable field.
- **A required institution set with loose positions (generator assumption).** Every county seat gets a yamen, a Confucian temple/school and a City God temple. This research does not establish that set as universal: the City God temple rests on the 1370 decree (secondary evidence), and the yamen and Confucian temple rest on single cases only (see Not found). The yamen is on the highest ground or central, and the City God temple is near the yamen (often behind it). The Confucian temple may sit outside the wall in small or early towns. Positions are soft constraints, not fixed slots.
- **The era switch: ward city vs street city.** Pre-Song (or "ward" style): walled wards of 0.5-1.1 km per side, two or four ward gates, two enclosed markets. Song and later: no ward walls, shops along streets, and suburbs (关厢) outside gates on the busiest road or landing.
- **Street-width hierarchy for planned capitals.** Use the Dadu ratio 24:12:6:3 bu (36/18/9/4.5 m). Ordinary county seats do not get this; their widths were not found in this research.
