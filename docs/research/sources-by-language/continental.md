## French, German, Italian and Dutch sources on town form

Scope: what sources in French, German, Italian and Dutch say about the form of pre-1900 European towns, with the focus on measured numbers. Every number below was read in a fetched document unless it is marked "(unverified)". Translations are mine and are labelled "(translation: Claude)".

Jargon used below, explained once:
- **Plot** (FR *parcelle*, *ayral*, *localium*; DE *Hofstätte*, *Parzelle*; IT *lotto*; NL *erf*, *perceel*): the piece of land one household got to build on.
- **Block** (FR *îlot*, *moulon*; IT *isolato*; NL *bouwblok*): land enclosed by streets on all sides.
- **Frontage**: the width of a plot along the street.
- **Bastide**: a planned new town in south-west France, mostly 1220-1370.
- **Terra nuova** (plural *terre nuove*): a planned new town founded by Florence, 1299-1350.
- **Gründungsstadt**: German "founded town", a town laid out by a lord on purpose (classic period 1150-1250).
- **Charter of customs** (FR *charte de coutumes*): the founding legal text of a bastide, which often fixes plot size and rent.
- **Venelle / andrône**: a very narrow gap between the backs or sides of houses, used for drains and as a fire break.

### Findings

1. **Founded towns used a fixed plot unit, written into the charter.** In Zähringer towns (south-west Germany and Switzerland) the unit was the *Hofstätte* of 50 x 100 Fuß in Freiburg im Breisgau, about 16.2 x 32.4 m. In Gascon bastides the charter of Rabastens-de-Bigorre fixes the taxed house plot at 60 x 20 *rases* (unit not converted; see Not found). A French archivist gives the average bastide plot as about 8 x 24 m, 150-200 m².
2. **The unit was real, but it did not stay clean.** Archaeology in Freiburg shows the 16.2 m plot width on the ground, with a spread of 14-18 m in width and 32-40 m in depth. By about 1200, new areas no longer followed the unit, and old plots were split. Löbbecke says the same thing happened in Bern and Burgdorf.
3. **The "one ideal Zähringer town" idea is contested.** German research from the 1920s-1950s (Hamm, Noack, Gruber) saw a common Zähringer plan: street market, a cross of main streets, uniform plots. From the 1960s Schwineköper and Meckseper rejected that: Bern, Burgdorf and Fribourg have no such cross, and Freiburg's four main streets are not at right angles. A generator should not treat "Zähringer cross" as a fixed template.
4. **Southern French planned towns used a "binary" block (FR *parcellaire binaire*).** Gilles Séraphin describes long, narrow blocks, 11.5-17 m wide and 50-70 m long, split down the middle by a gap under 0.5 m wide. Bigger "head houses" stand on the main street at the block ends; small row houses face the side lanes. Streets come in three ranks: main street, cross lane about half as wide, and the sub-0.5 m back gap. He finds this pattern from Figeac to Villefranche-de-Rouergue, and in Bilbao, Guernica and Vitoria at a larger size (blocks about 30 x 80 m).
5. **Bastide market squares grew over time.** Pierre Gérard gives early bastide squares of 50-55 m a side, rising to 60 m (Monflanquin 1268), 70 m (Cologne-du-Gers 1286), 75 m (Grenade 1290), 90 m (Revel 1342), and long rectangles of 70 x 140 m (Marciac 1298) and 75 x 150 m (Rabastens 1306).
6. **Florentine new towns graded everything outward from one main street.** The main street (*via maestra*) runs gate to gate. Streets, blocks, building heights, and lot depths decrease towards the walls. Sources disagree on whether lot width also decreased (Italian Wikipedia: yes; Museo delle Terre Nuove: width stayed constant, depth changed). San Giovanni Valdarno has a measured scheme: walls 321 x 800 braccia (2:5 ratio), a 20-braccia road inside the wall, and a 37.5-braccia strip outside for moat and road.
7. **German market forms are regional.** In Old Bavaria, towns had either a widened through-road as market (*Straßenmarkt*: Aichach, Deggendorf, Landshut, Straubing and others) or a square market (*eckiger Marktplatz*: Dingolfing, Burghausen, Friedberg, Landsberg am Lech). A Breslau conference summary says squares were most often rectangular, sometimes square, and triangular squares grew from a fork of two roads; triangles are not always older than rectangles (Brno).
8. **German typology by founding period (Stoob).** *Altstädte* before 1150 (often Roman roots), *Gründungsstädte* 1150-1250, *Kleinstädte* 1250-1300, *Minderstädte* ("lesser towns", partial urban rights) after 1300.
9. **Dutch towns were dense and got denser.** J.C. Visser computes average density of Dutch towns of 75.5 inhabitants per hectare in 1400 and 126.5 in 1560. Amsterdam was over 300/ha in 1560. Dordrecht and Nijmegen passed 200/ha on about 50 ha. Small, generously walled towns sat low: Asperen about 40/ha.
10. **Dutch plots were narrow and graded by canal.** In the Amsterdam canal ring (from 1613) a Prinsengracht plot was 22 Amsterdam feet (6.2 m) wide; expensive Herengracht plots were 30 feet (8.5 m).
11. **Dutch planned towns often reused a polder-village skeleton.** Willemstad looks like a single fortress design, but J. Renes shows its street plan (a broad *Voorstraat* from dike to church, a *kerkring* round the church, two back streets) is the older polder village of Ruygenhil (1564); the star fortress came later (1583-1601).
12. **Italian communal rules regulated building fronts.** Bologna from 1288 required new houses to have arcades (*portici*) at least 7 Bolognese feet (2.66 m) high and as wide.

### Numbers

Unit conversions used (factor and source):
- Freiburg Fuß = 0.324 m. Löbbecke: "Der Freiburger Fuß maß vermutlich 32,4 cm" (translation: Claude: "The Freiburg foot probably measured 32.4 cm"). Gutmann claims the same foot for all Zähringer foundations; that claim is from a local-history article and is weaker.
- Florentine braccio (*braccio da panno*) = 0.5836 m; *pertica* = 5 braccia. Source: Bartoli 2019.
- Amsterdam voet = 0.283133 m; Amsterdam roede = 3.68 m (13 feet). Rijnland voet = 0.314 m; Rijnland roede = 3.767 m. Source: English Wikipedia, Dutch units of measurement (secondary). Stadsherstel's own figures (22 ft = 6.2 m, 30 ft = 8.5 m) give 0.282-0.283 m, consistent.
- Toise de Paris = 1.949 m = 6 pieds, so pied du roi = 0.3248 m. Source: French Wikipedia, Toise (the pied value is my division).
- Bolognese foot: 7 piedi = 2.66 m per Italian Wikipedia, so 1 piede = 0.38 m (my division).
- Gascon *rase*: conversion not found.

### Case studies

1. **Freiburg im Breisgau (Zähringer foundation, 12th century).** The 1218 town law says: "Und soll jegliche Hofstätte sein hundert Schuh lang und fünfzig breit" (translation: Claude: "And every house plot shall be a hundred feet long and fifty wide"). Löbbecke's conclusion: "Das Hofstättenmaß von 50 x 100 Fuß (16,20 x 32,40 m) wurde in Freiburg im 12. Jahrhundert als reales Grundstücksmaß genutzt. Es diente also nicht nur als Berechungseinheit für die Grundsteuer." (translation: Claude: "The plot measure of 50 x 100 feet (16.20 x 32.40 m) was used in Freiburg in the 12th century as a real plot size. So it was not only a unit for calculating ground rent.") He adds that growth pressure around 1200 broke the norm: new areas did not follow it, and old large plots were split. Lesson: start with uniform large plots, then split them over time.
2. **The Zähringer debate.** Löbbecke summarises: Schwineköper "negierte das ‚Zähringerkreuz': Die ebenfalls von den Zähringern gegründeten Schweizer Städte Bern, Burgdorf und Freiburg im Üchtland besitzen kein solches Straßenkreuz" (translation: Claude: "rejected the 'Zähringer cross': the Swiss towns Bern, Burgdorf and Fribourg, also founded by the Zähringer, have no such street cross"). He links the earlier uniform-grid thesis to the 1920s fashion for grids in art and architecture. Lesson: a "founded town" pack should allow several skeletons, not one.
3. **Stollhofen (Baden).** A local historian argues the town was laid out with 1000-Fuß measuring cords and circle arcs, with about 60 equal plots of about 50 x 100 Fuß (16.2 x 32.4 m, "ca. 5,3 ar"), houses gable-on to the main street, plots reached by passages or back lanes, and the church "etwas abseits in einer Seitengasse" (translation: Claude: "somewhat aside in a side lane"). Two main streets formed a street market planned larger than needed ("man plante auf Zuwachs": "they planned for growth"). Treat the circle-arc method as a hypothesis, not established fact.
4. **Figeac, Ortabadial quarter (Lot).** Séraphin: "Entre les ruelles, les îlots très étirés ont une largeur de 11,50 m à 12 m pour une longueur de près de 50 m. Ils sont recoupés dans leur axe par une venelle de moins de 0,50 m de largeur, impropre à la circulation." (translation: Claude: "Between the lanes, the very elongated blocks are 11.50 to 12 m wide and nearly 50 m long. They are cut along their axis by a back gap less than 0.50 m wide, unusable for traffic.") Three house types follow from this: head houses on the main street, row houses on the lanes, and corner doubles.
5. **Villefranche-de-Rouergue (1250-52).** The binary block applied to a whole bastide, "en le systématisant jusqu'à l'illogisme" (translation: Claude: "systematising it to the point of illogic"), because the binary block is one-directional and the town has two perpendicular axes. About 140 blocks; streets 5.5-6 m; lanes 2-3 m; four streets meet at an approximately square market place.
6. **Bastides of south-west France in general (Gérard).** "Chaque parcelle (localium, platea, ayral), présentant une largeur sur rue de 4 à 10 mètres, est plus ou moins longue selon les lieux, les dimensions moyennes étant d'environ 8 x 24 mètres." (translation: Claude: "Each plot (localium, platea, ayral), with a street frontage of 4 to 10 metres, is more or less long depending on the place, the average size being about 8 x 24 metres.") Settlers also got a garden and fields outside. Grenade offered 3000 house plots; barely a third were taken. Lesson: planned capacity and actual fill differ.
7. **San Giovanni Valdarno (Florence, about 1299).** Bartoli: "Tutt'intorno alle mura correva all'interno una strada di uniforme larghezza (20 br.) e all'esterno una striscia di terra dedicata al fossato e alla via intorno ad esso (37,5 br.)" (translation: Claude: "All around the walls ran, inside, a street of uniform width (20 braccia) and, outside, a strip of land given to the moat and the road around it (37.5 braccia)"). The wall rectangle is 2:5. Four quarters of four blocks each sit around the cross of the main street and a wide transverse piazza.
8. **Florentine terre nuove in general.** Treccani describes "l'armonico diminuire della larghezza delle strade e degli isolati" (translation: Claude: "the harmonious decrease in the width of streets and blocks") away from the central axis. The Museo delle Terre Nuove: "la strada principale ospitava lotti più profondi, cioè più grandi, mentre le strade secondarie erano meno profonde ... anche se la larghezza dei lotti restava sempre la stessa" (translation: Claude: "the main street had deeper, that is larger, lots, while the secondary streets had shallower ones ... although lot width always stayed the same"). Italian Wikipedia instead says width and depth both decreased. Treccani links shallow lots to a tax based on frontage only.
9. **Bavarian market towns (Liebhart).** "Das Einstraßensystem mit einer einfachen Häuserreihe, Giebel an Giebel, gehörte zur Grundausstattung. Eine Querstraße bildete ein Straßenkreuz, das die Siedlung in Viertel teilte." (translation: Claude: "The single-street system with a simple row of houses, gable to gable, was the basic equipment. A cross street formed a street cross that divided the settlement into quarters.") "Hier wurden Durchgangsstraßen zu Plätzen ausgeweitet." (translation: Claude: "Here through-roads were widened into squares.") A market place was the main thing that distinguished a town from a village.
10. **Amsterdam canal ring (1613).** Stadsherstel: "De breedte van een perceel was er 22 Amsterdamse voet (6,2 meter) ... De dure percelen op de Herengracht hadden maar liefst een breedte ... van 30 voet (8,5 meter)." (translation: Claude: "The width of a plot there [Prinsengracht] was 22 Amsterdam feet (6.2 m) ... The expensive plots on the Herengracht had a width of no less than 30 feet (8.5 m).") Prinsengracht was a living-and-working canal with warehouses; Herengracht and Keizersgracht were for the rich. Plot width signals status by street.
11. **Willemstad (1564/1583).** Renes: "Bij nadere beschouwing blijkt het stratenplan ouder dan de vesting en blijken ook de vestingwerken niet in een keer aangelegd te zijn." (translation: Claude: "On closer inspection the street plan turns out to be older than the fortress, and the fortifications were not built all at once either.") The polder-village type: a broad *voorstraat* closed by the dike at one end and the church at the other, a ring road round the church, and parallel back streets serving the rear of the plots.
12. **Dutch densities (Visser 1985).** "Uit deze cijfers blijkt, dat in de periode 1400-1560 een sterke verdichting van de bevolking in de steden heeft plaatsgevonden." (translation: Claude: "These figures show that in 1400-1560 the towns' population became much denser.") He warns that average densities cannot be used to compute one town's population from its area; they are only a check.

### What this adds to the English-language research

- **A second plot model besides the English burgage.** The English burgage (narrow, very deep) is one option. Continental founded towns used much wider plots (Freiburg 16.2 m) that were later split, and southern French towns used the binary block with shallow houses (about 6 m deep) and a sub-0.5 m back gap. These produce visibly different block textures on a map.
- **Plot size written in the founding charter.** Freiburg (1218), Bern (unverified) and Rabastens-de-Bigorre all fix a standard plot and its rent in the town law. This supports "plot unit" as a spec-level parameter, not a derived value.
- **Strong grading from the main street outward** in Florentine new towns: street width, block width, lot depth and building height all fall towards the wall. This is a clear, data-friendly rule.
- **Measured market-square sizes** for bastides (50-90 m squares, up to 75 x 150 m rectangles), and a regional split in Germany between street markets (south) and square markets (north-west; the Meckseper map is unverified, seen only in a search snippet).
- **Hard density numbers for the Low Countries**: 75 inh./ha (1400) to 127 inh./ha (1560) on average, 150-300+ in big towns, about 40 in an over-walled small town. These are walled-area densities, comparable to the British figures if those are also per walled hectare.
- **Scholarly caution.** German and French authors stress that modern cadastres show altered plots, that a "Zähringer type" is partly a 1920s idea, and that triangular markets are not always older than square ones. The English-language corpus tended to present types as settled.

### What this means for a generator and rule packs

- Add a **plot-unit** field to a rule pack (width x depth, in a named historic unit with a metre factor), plus a **split rule** (for example: halve frontage after N growth steps). Freiburg (50 x 100 Fuß, split from about 1200) and the bastides (about 8 x 24 m) are two calibration points.
- Add a **block-type** choice with at least three closed-enum values: burgage strip (English), wide Hofstätte with later splits (German founded town), binary block with a centre gap under 0.5 m (southern French). Each type fixes how houses face streets and lanes.
- Add a **street-rank gradient** rule for planned towns: main street widest, then parallel streets, then cross lanes, then back gaps (Figeac: 4-4.5 / 2-2.4 / under 0.5 m; Villefranche: 5.5-6 / 2-3 m; San Giovanni: 20-braccia wall road). For Florentine packs, lot depth falls with distance from the main axis.
- Add a **market form** enum: street market (widened through-road), rectangular square, triangular square at a road fork. Bastide square sides of 50-90 m give a size range.
- Use **density bands per walled hectare** as a validation check, not as a generator input: about 40-95 inh./ha for small or over-walled towns, 120-200 for mid-sized towns, 300+ only for a boom city like Amsterdam in 1560.
- For Dutch packs, offer the **polder-village skeleton** (dike, broad *voorstraat*, church ring, back streets) as a seed that a later fortress wraps around, as in Willemstad.
