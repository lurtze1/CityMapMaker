## Arabic, Persian and Ottoman sources on town form

Scope: original-language legal texts and measured numbers on how towns in the Islamic world were laid out, to correct the English-only and Europe-heavy bias of earlier research. Searched in Arabic, Persian, Turkish, French and English on 2026-10-20.

How to read this report:
- Every claim has a fetched URL. A claim seen only in a search-result snippet is marked "(unverified)".
- Quotes in Arabic, Persian or Turkish are short. Each has a translation labelled "(translation: Claude)".
- Three Arabic texts (Ibn al-Rami, Ibn Abdun, al-Saqati) were read from archive.org OCR (machine-read text from page scans). The Ibn al-Rami OCR is mostly clean. The Ibn Abdun OCR is badly garbled; quotes from it are my reading of garbled text, and I mark them "(OCR reading)". Square brackets [...] mark a gap I could not read.
- Jargon at first use: **hisba** = the office of market and street inspection; **muhtasib** = the inspector; **fina'** (فناء) = the strip of street in front of a house that the owner may use but not own; **darb / zuqaq ghayr nafidh** = dead-end lane; **mahalle / mahalleh / hara** = residential quarter; **tahrir defteri** = Ottoman tax-survey register; **zira / arşın** = Ottoman builder's cubit; **dhira'** (ذراع) = Arabic cubit; **rūn** (رون) = Persian system of building orientation.

### Findings

1. **The "seven cubits" rule is real, and it is a dispute rule, not a planning standard.** The hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari 2473 and Sahih Muslim 1613 says that when people disagree about a road, it is set at seven cubits. Maliki jurists applied it when someone built into an existing street: Ibn al-Rami (Tunis, 1330s) reports one view that a building jutting into a street narrower than seven cubits is pulled down. Seven legal cubits is about 3.5 m (factor below).
2. **Andalusian jurists gave a three-class street table.** Isa ibn Musa (quoted by Ibn al-Rami) reports: footpaths 7 cubits, livestock roads 20 cubits, and a side lane that does not join a known road 4 cubits. That is about 3.5 m, 10 m and 2 m. Ibn Kinana gives a functional rule instead: a lane must pass the largest thing that uses it, such as a camel with its biggest litter. Ibn Habib adds that this applies to newly laid-out building, not to old fabric.
3. **"Wide street" had a number.** Ibn 'At's al-Turar, quoted by Ibn al-Rami, defines a wide street as 9 cubits (about 4.5 m). A lane under 7 cubits counts as narrow; in a narrow lane you may not open a door facing your neighbour's door.
4. **Dead-end lanes were collectively owned by their residents.** Sahnun's ruling, quoted by Ibn al-Rami: nobody may open a new door into a dead-end lane without the consent of all its residents. A second view only needs the consent of residents further in, whose route passes the new door.
5. **Height was not protected; privacy was.** Ibn 'Attab, quoted by Ibn al-Rami: all harm to a neighbour is removed, except raising a building that blocks wind and sunlight. Looking into a neighbour's entrance hall from a shop is a serious harm, but it is tolerated in a market or beside a very wide, busy street.
6. **The fina' could be used, not built on.** Caliph Umar assigned the afniya to house owners. Asbagh would let an owner enclose it if the road behind stays wide. Ibn Habib limits it to sitting, tethering animals and small-scale selling, not enclosure by building.
7. **Hisba manuals set market and street order.** Al-Shayzari (Syria, 12th century): markets as high and wide as the Romans built them; raised walkways on both sides where unpaved; no shop bench beyond the line of the arcade posts; each trade its own market; trades that use fire (bakers, cooks, smiths) kept away from perfumers and cloth sellers; no wall or shop pushed into the usual passage of streets and quarter lanes; roof spouts replaced by channels cut into the wall; house sewage outlets closed in summer and a cesspit dug inside the house; no looking at neighbours from roofs and windows.
8. **Ibn Abdun (Seville, c. 1100) pushes nuisances outside the gates** (OCR reading): bricks and roof tiles are made outside the city gates; no dung or refuse is dumped inside the city, only outside the gates in fields and gardens or at set dumping places.
9. **Ottoman Istanbul codified street classes after 1839, and banned dead ends.** The 17 May 1839 instruction (ilmühaber) set main routes at 20 zira (15.20 m) with trees and 4-zira (3.04 m) pavements, other roads at least 10 zira (7.60 m), and no dead-end streets. The 1848/49 Ebniye Nizamnamesi used 12, 10, 6 zira (9, 7.5, 4.5 m) and ordered existing dead ends removed. The 1863 Turuk ve Ebniye Nizamnamesi used 15, 12, 10, 8 zira (11.25, 9, 7.5, 6 m). After the 1865 Hocapaşa fire, roads were set at 6 to 25 arşın and 25% of each plot was taken without payment for widening.
10. **Measured densities for Arab cities sit around 300–600 inhabitants per hectare.** Cairo c. 1798: 263,000 people on 730 ha = 360/ha; the walled Qahira core 588/ha. Tunis 1881: 380/ha. Raymond rejects higher figures (Aleppo 710/ha, Algiers 2,433/ha) as improbable.
11. **Dead ends were a large share of the street network.** Raymond: in the old quarters of Fez, Cairo and Aleppo, dead ends are 52%, 47% and 41% of total street length.
12. **Quarter sizes are measurable.** Ottoman Cairo: about 70 quarters, about 2 ha and about 800 people (150–200 families) each. Ottoman Aleppo (tax surveys): 72 quarters in 1537, average 145 housing units each, 8 persons per unit. The 1871 Ottoman provincial law defined a mahalle as at least 50 households.
13. **Safavid Isfahan's royal square is 560 × 160 m**, ringed by 200 two-storey shop units, with the Shah Mosque turned off the square's axis to face the qibla (the direction of Mecca).
14. **Persian orientation tradition.** Pirnia's three rūns: rūn-e rāsteh (qibla) on a NE–SW axis, rūn-e Esfahāni on a NW–SE axis, rūn-e Kermāni on an E–W axis. A modern historian (Heydari Delgarm 2017) traces the idea back to old cosmology and solar-solstice axes, not only climate.

Unit conversions used throughout:
- **Arabic legal cubit (dhira' shar'i): 0.498 m.** Source: Wikipedia "Cubit", citing Hinz, "Dhirāʿ", Encyclopaedia of Islam 2nd ed., II, 231–232: "It measured 49.8 cm". Abbasid variant 48.25 cm; "black cubit" 54.04 cm. So 7 cubits = 3.49 m (legal) to 3.78 m (black). I use 0.498 below unless a source states otherwise. One Arabic hadith-commentary site gives 69 cm per cubit (7 cubits = 4.8 m); it cites no source, so I do not use it.
- **Ottoman builder's arşın / zira: 0.758 m.** Source: Wikipedia "Arşın", citing Özdural 1998 (Muqarnas 15): "75.774 cm on average". The Turkish sources below round it to 0.75 m (Yörüten & Hamamcıoğlu) or 0.76 m (G. Çelik, Engin).

### Case studies

1. **Tunis, 1330s: Ibn al-Rami's al-I'lan bi-ahkam al-bunyan.** Ibn al-Rami was a working builder and court expert. His book collects Maliki rulings from Qayrawan, Cordoba and Tunis on walls, harm to neighbours, defects and planting. For a map, the useful rules are: the 7-cubit encroachment threshold (§152), the 9-cubit "wide street" (§134), consent of all residents before a new door in a dead end (§141), no protection of light or wind against a neighbour's higher building (§139), and use-but-not-build on the fina' (§165–166). Read from archive.org OCR of Farid Ben Slimane's critical edition (the publication year is not shown in the OCR I read).
2. **Al-Andalus: a three-class street table.** Isa ibn Musa (al-Tutili) reports 7 cubits for footpaths, 20 for livestock roads, 4 for a side lane (§399). Ibn Kinana and Ibn Habib add a functional test (a loaded camel must pass) and limit it to new layouts (§400). This is the only original-language source I found that ties width to street function.
3. **Syria, 12th century: al-Shayzari's market chapter.** Chapter 2, "on inspecting markets and streets", is a short zoning code: arcade line as a build-to line, one market per trade, fire trades away from perfumers and cloth, no projection into quarter lanes, wall-cut drains, no overlooking from roofs. The 1946 editor's footnote adds that the shop bench (mastaba) was about one metre high and that Muhammad Ali ordered them removed in Cairo in 1250 AH / 1835 so they would not narrow the street.
4. **Seville, c. 1100: Ibn Abdun.** The OCR is poor, but two rules are clear enough: brick and tile kilns outside the gates (the reason given is lack of space in the city), and refuse dumped only outside the gates or at fixed dumping places. The same 1955 volume has a section, probably by Ibn Abd al-Ra'uf (attribution from its position in the OCR, unverified), that keeps oven-keepers and glassmakers from storing firewood near the fire.
5. **Cairo c. 1798: densities and quarters (Raymond).** From the Description de l'Égypte map: 730 ha, 360 people per ha overall, 588 in the walled core. About 70 quarters of about 2 ha and 800 people each, gated at night. Dead ends were 47% of street length. Ordinary streets 2–3 m wide; the Fatimid spine about 10 m.
6. **Aleppo 1537–1683: Ottoman tax surveys (Raymond).** 72–74 quarters; average 145 housing units per quarter; 8 persons per unit. Intramural quarters stayed at 22–24 and about 5,100 units for 150 years while suburbs nearly doubled (4,481 → 8,743 units). Built-up area grew from 238.5 to 349 ha by the early 19th century. Blocks about 45 × 125 m; dead ends 41% of street length. The tanneries were moved outside the walls before 1574, about 200 m from the Antioch Gate.
7. **Istanbul 1839–1882: Tanzimat street codes.** A sequence of codes (1839, 1848/49, 1863, 1875, 1882) set width classes, height caps tied to material, and a ban on dead ends. The codes kept being reissued because, per Denel (cited by Yörüten & Hamamcıoğlu), people did not comply and the administration made concessions. After fires, burned areas were re-mapped and regridded; dead ends were removed in nearly all replanned fire zones.
8. **Isfahan 1602: Naqsh-e Jahan.** A planned 560 × 160 m square lined with 200 two-storey shop units, with the Shah Mosque, Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque, Ali Qapu and the Qeysariyeh bazaar gate at the midpoints of the four sides. The square's axis does not face the qibla; the mosque corrects this in its entrance passages. It linked the old square (Meydan-e Kohneh) to the new one by extending the bazaar.
9. **Iran: rūn orientation.** Pirnia's three orientations (NE–SW "rāsteh/qibla", NW–SE "Esfahāni", E–W "Kermāni") are standard in Persian architecture teaching. Heydari Delgarm (2017) shows that the hexagon behind the rūns comes from solstice sunrise and sunset axes, which make exactly 60° angles at about 39° N. A Persian search snippet (unverified) says Qajar houses in Isfahan rarely follow rūn-e Esfahāni; plot, access and lane direction mattered more.
10. **Yazd: neighbourhood spaces (qualitative only).** Abbasi Harofteh & Sadeghian (2020) find three semi-public patterns in Yazd's historic fabric: entrance-axis, darband-axis (a gated lane) and sabat-axis (a vaulted passage built over the lane). No measurements in the abstract.

### What this adds to the English-language research

- **The 7-cubit rule is narrower than Hakim's summary suggests.** In the original it is a dispute and encroachment rule. Jurists disagreed about it, and Ibn Habib limits the functional width rule to newly founded quarters. It is not a minimum width for every lane: Isa ibn Musa allows 4-cubit side lanes.
- **There is a street hierarchy in the law itself**: 4 / 7 / 9 / 20 cubits (side lane, footpath, wide street, livestock road). The English summaries I had seen give only the 7.
- **Dead ends were legally private to their residents**, which explains why they persist and why the Ottoman codes had to ban them by statute.
- **Height was free; overlooking was not.** This contradicts the common English claim that Islamic law protected neighbours' light. Ibn 'Attab protects privacy, not light or wind.
- **Hard numbers from French scholarship (Raymond)**: densities, quarter sizes, dead-end shares, block sizes and tannery distances. The earlier English work had few of these.
- **The Ottoman 19th century is a separate "rule pack"**: graded widths in zira, material-linked height caps, a dead-end ban and post-fire regridding. It is a modernising code, not traditional Islamic practice.
- **Persian planning used orientation systems (rūn) and planned royal squares.** That is a design tradition, not only "organic" growth.

### What this means for a generator and rule packs

- **Add a "Maliki/Maghreb" rule pack with a street-class table in cubits**: side lane 4 (2.0 m), footpath 7 (3.5 m), wide street 9 (4.5 m), livestock/main road 20 (10 m). Store the cubit length as pack data (0.498 m legal; 0.540 m black cubit) so a pack can switch it.
- **Model dead-end lanes as owned by their residents.** Ban new doors facing each other in lanes under 7 cubits. As a calibration target, dead ends should be 40–52% of street length in old Arab-city quarters (Aleppo 41%, Cairo 47%, Fez 52%).
- **Quarter (mahalle) sizing**: about 2 ha, 150–200 households, 800–1,200 people per quarter, density 300–600 people per ha. The 1871 Ottoman minimum is 50 households.
- **Zoning by nuisance**: one market per trade; fire trades away from perfumers and cloth; brick and tile kilns, refuse and tanneries outside the gates. Tanneries move outward as the city grows (Aleppo before 1574, Cairo c. 1600, Tunis c. 1770).
- **A separate "Ottoman Tanzimat (post-1839)" pack**: zira width classes (for example 1863: 15/12/10/8 zira, minimum 6 zira), a dead-end ban, height caps by material, and a regridded fire zone. It must not be mixed with the traditional pack.
- **A Persian pack option**: a planned rectangular royal square (Naqsh-e Jahan ratio 3.5:1) with the main mosque rotated toward the qibla, and a pack-level grid orientation from the three rūns. Who decides which rūn a city uses: Jeffrey (optional pack parameter).
