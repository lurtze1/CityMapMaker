## Spanish and Portuguese sources on town form

Scope: what Spanish- and Portuguese-language scholarship says about historic town form in Iberia and
Latin America, with measured numbers. Research date: 2026-10-20. Every number below comes from a
fetched page or PDF unless it is marked "(unverified)", which means it was seen only in a search
snippet. Translations of quoted passages are mine and are labelled "(translation: Claude)".

### Findings

1. **The 1573 Ordinances fix the plaza, not the grid.** In the original Spanish text (Altamira's
   transcription of the Archivo General de Indias copy), the town-planning ordinances 110 to 137 give
   numbers only for the main plaza (ordinance 113) and for two lot types (ordinances 105 and 106).
   They give no block size, no street width in feet, and no number of lots per block. Ordinance 116
   says streets are wide in cold places and narrow in hot places, but the page is torn ("Roto") at
   that point. Every measured grid in Spanish America therefore came from local practice, not from
   the law.
   - Ordinance 112: plaza "sea en quadro prolongada que por lo menos tenga de largo Vna vez y media
     de su ancho". "Be a lengthened square, at least one and a half times as long as it is wide"
     (translation: Claude).
   - Ordinance 113: plaza no smaller than "[roto]…os pies en ancho y treçientos de largo ni mayor de
     ochoçientos pies de largo y quinientos y treynta pies de ancho; de mediana y de buena
     proporçion es de seisçientos pies de largo y quatroçientos de ancho". "[torn]… feet wide and
     three hundred long, nor larger than eight hundred feet long and five hundred and thirty wide; a
     middling and well-proportioned one is six hundred feet long and four hundred wide"
     (translation: Claude). The minimum width is torn in this copy. The English HUD translation is
     said to read 200 feet, but I could not open that PDF (unverified).
   - Ordinance 114: four main streets leave from the middle of each side and two from each corner
     (eight corner streets in all). The plaza corners face the four main winds.
   - Ordinance 115: the plaza and the four main streets have portales (arcades). The eight corner
     streets reach the plaza free of arcades.
   - Ordinances 118, 119, 124, 126: smaller plazas are placed "a trechos" (at intervals) for parish
     churches and monasteries. Churches get a whole block ("ysla entera"). Inland, the main church
     is not on the plaza but near it and raised on steps. No private lots are given on the plaza.
   - Ordinances 122, 123: butchers, fish markets and tanneries are placed where their filth can be
     removed, downstream on a river.
   - Ordinance 129: the ejido (town common) must be large enough that the town can keep growing.
   - Ordinance 134: buildings "sean de vna forma por el ornato de la poblaçion". "Be of one form,
     for the town's beauty" (translation: Claude).
   - Ordinances 105, 106: "Es vna peonia solar de çincuenta pies en ancho y çiento en largo"; "Vna
     caualleria es solar para cassa de çien pies de ancho y doçientos de largo". "A peonía is a house
     lot fifty feet wide and a hundred long"; "a caballería is a house lot a hundred feet wide and
     two hundred long" (translation: Claude). A peonía went to a foot soldier, a caballería to a
     horseman.

2. **Real Spanish American grids are large and coarse.** Measured blocks run from about 115 m to
   over 200 m on a side, with streets of 9.5 to 13.3 m (Puebla, Mexico City, Santiago, Lima,
   Buenos Aires; see the Numbers table). Blocks were split into 4 lots (Lima, Santiago, Buenos
   Aires) or 8 lots (Puebla). Early lots were therefore huge, often a quarter block of 50 to 60 m on
   a side.

3. **Colonial grids filled in slowly and densified by subdivision.** Mexico City in 1794 was still
   53.77% open space (patios, gardens, atria) inside a 249.9 ha study area (Rena 2019). In Buenos
   Aires the city "no expandió territorialmente … sino que su edificación ganó en densidad … se
   subdividieron los primitivos solares de un cuarto de manzana" (Camarda 2008, quoting an older
   author). "Did not expand in area … its building gained density … the original quarter-block lots
   were subdivided" (translation: Claude). In 1744 the blocks next to the plaza held 100 to 200
   people each.

4. **The Portuguese tradition is built on a different lot.** Walter Rossa (Coimbra) contrasts two
   lot types. The Spanish damero (checkerboard) is cut into square or near-square lots for a
   courtyard house, so all four block faces are alike. The Portuguese and northern European
   "lote estreito e comprido" (narrow, long lot) puts the house on the street front and the yard
   behind. That makes rectangular blocks with a street hierarchy: ruas (front streets) one way and
   travessas (cross lanes) the other. Rossa says this lot adapts to steep ground far better than a
   grid of courtyard houses. Measured modules: Bairro Alto, Lisbon (early 16th century), 30 × 60
   palmos; Vila Real de Santo António (1774), 24 × 50 palmos.

5. **Upper town and lower town.** Portuguese sources describe the cidade alta (upper town: castle,
   cathedral, government) and cidade baixa (lower town: port, trade) as two linked settlements.
   Salvador (1549) is the clearest case: an escarpment of about 60 m, one long winding street along
   the shore, a second one along the clifftop, seven paved slopes and four cargo hoists between
   them. Rossa describes the same pattern in Coimbra: the Alta (hill) emptied out as the centre
   moved down to the Baixa, the narrow strip between hill and river.

6. **Islamic Iberia: the dead-end lane is a sign of crowding, not a founding rule.** Navarro
   Palazón and Jiménez Castillo (CSIC) argue that early Andalusi towns were sparse. Houses stood
   among gardens inside walls built with room to grow (the "medina dispersa"). Dead-end lanes
   (adarves) appear when the town fills up: large houses are split, and the new inner houses need
   an access lane. A 2025 excavation in Valencia (La Boatella, c. 1150 to 1238) adds a twist: there
   the adarves were planned from the start, in a laid-out suburb. Measured houses there average
   88 to 94 m², rooms about 20 m², and secondary streets 2.2 to 3.2 m. Third-order lanes elsewhere
   (Saltés, Huelva) were 1.4 to 1.6 m.

7. **Christian new towns (villas nuevas, pueblas) used small modules.** A drone-and-cadastre survey
   of medieval new towns in Castile and León (Sáinz Guerra 2014) finds recurring block widths of
   about 25 m (30 varas), 45 m (54 varas) and 59 m (70 varas), streets of 4 to 5 varas (3.3 to
   4.2 m), and a main street of about 8 varas (6.7 m). Founding charters in Álava fix house lots of
   4 × 12 and 3 × 15 estados (about 6.7 × 20 m and 5 × 25 m). Lots are long and narrow, like the
   Portuguese lot.

8. **Pre-Columbian cities, as Latin American scholars report them.** Teotihuacan had more than
   2,000 apartment compounds of 30 to 100 m per side and a building unit of about 84 cm (Florescano,
   citing Millon and Sugiyama). Tenochtitlan grew from an island of about 180 ha (c. 1325) to about
   750 ha by 1519, with residential plots of 100 to 500 m² (Valero de García Lascuráin 1990). Chan
   Chan covered more than 20 km² with a 6 km² core and 10 walled compounds (ciudadelas). In Cusco,
   the Spanish handed out solares of "doscientos pies" in 1534 inside the Inca canchas (walled
   lineage compounds); Garcilaso lists 22 barrios, 9 in the sacred core and 13 in the outer ring.

### Case studies

**1. Puebla de los Ángeles (1531): the strict Spanish grid.** Rafael López Guzmán (Universidad de
Granada) describes "una parrilla rectangular de 100 x 200 varas castellanas", with eight lots per
block and streets of 14 varas. "A rectangular grid of 100 × 200 Castilian varas" (translation:
Claude). The plaza takes one full block plus the width of the eight corner streets. Puebla predates
the 1573 law yet already uses the corner-street rule later written into ordinance 114.

**2. Mexico City (1521–1524): a grid laid over the Aztec causeways.** Antonio Rena (2019) gives
blocks of 250 × 150 varas and streets of 16 varas on average. "Las manzanas en su mayoría fueron
rectangulares y de grandes dimensiones". "Most blocks were rectangular and very large"
(translation: Claude). The plaza covers 2.07 ha. In 1794 more than half the centre was still open
space inside the blocks. The search snippets name Alonso García Bravo as the surveyor and say the
grid followed the pre-Hispanic causeways; Rena confirms García Bravo.

**3. Santiago de Chile (1541): the Lima model, 138 + 12.** Memoria Chilena (Chilean National
Library) gives "ciento veintiséis manzanas regulares de ciento treinta y ocho varas de longitud,
separadas por calles de doce varas de ancho". "One hundred twenty-six regular blocks of 138 varas,
separated by streets twelve varas wide" (translation: Claude). Block plus street is 150 varas,
about 125 m from street centre to street centre.

**4. Buenos Aires (1580): quarter-block lots that were later split.** Garay's plan had a large
checkerboard of square blocks around the Plaza Mayor. Each settler got one urban lot of a quarter
block, plus a block for a garden and land outside. By 1744 the blocks next to the plaza held 100
to 200 people each, mostly through subdivision and rented rooms (Camarda 2008). Block and street
sizes are only partly verified (see Numbers).

**5. Salvador da Bahia (1549): the Portuguese upper and lower town.** The heritage dossier says
Portugal "aliou à tradição da implantação das cidades em sítios elevados o traçado retilíneo em
quadras". "Joined the tradition of placing cities on high sites to a straight-line layout in
blocks" (translation: Claude). The upper town sits about 65 m above the sea. In 1551 a visitor found
the upper town "mui vazia, assim de casas e de gente" ("very empty, of houses and of people";
translation: Claude), while life went on in the lower town by the port. Around 1800 Vilhena
describes the lower town as "uma rua tortuosa, mas continuada" ("one winding but continuous
street"; translation: Claude), with buildings of three and four storeys. Seven paved slopes
(calçadas) and hoists linked the two levels.

**6. Lisbon Bairro Alto and Vila Real de Santo António: the Portuguese lot module.** Walter Rossa
reconstructs the Bairro Alto (a private speculative subdivision of the early 1500s) from a single
lot module: "um lote com 30x60 palmos, o chão a que se referem inúmeros documentos". "A lot of 30 ×
60 palmos, the 'chão' that countless documents mention" (translation: Claude). That is about
6.6 × 13.2 m, a double square. Streets follow the slope for drainage, in a "sistema de rua e
travessa". Two centuries later Vila Real de Santo António uses a 24 × 50 palmo lot and a 66 m
square plaza circle. Rossa also notes that in Spanish America "o damero que efetivamente se adotou
no terreno" was "uma versão extraordinariamente simplificada" of the legislated model, and lot
division "nem sempre regular ou modular". "The checkerboard actually laid out was an extremely
simplified version … not always regular or modular" (translation: Claude).

**7. Valencia, La Boatella (c. 1150–1238): a planned Islamic suburb with planned dead ends.**
Mateu, Herreros and García (2025) excavated 4,800 m² with up to 43 courtyard houses. Secondary
streets were 2.2 to 3.2 m wide, with straight continuous façades: "impiden pensar en una
urbanización espontánea". "Rule out spontaneous building" (translation: Claude). From them, lanes
branch at near right angles, some of them dead ends. "Muchos adarves eran, en consecuencia,
planificados". "Many dead-end lanes were therefore planned" (translation: Claude). House area
averages 88 to 94 m², described as homes of "gente de un sustrato más bien humilde" ("people of a
rather humble background"; translation: Claude).

**8. Murcia and Siyâsa: from dispersed to saturated medina.** Navarro Palazón and Jiménez Castillo
(2007) give the general model. Early walls enclosed "amplias zonas vacías" ("large empty areas";
translation: Claude) for growth. As towns filled, old plots were split, streets were narrowed and
even closed, and "se generan … adarves que garantizarán la entrada a las nuevas propiedades"
("dead-end lanes are created to give access to the new properties"; translation: Claude). Houses
then grew upwards. Siyâsa itself was abandoned around the third quarter of the 13th century,
which froze this process for excavators.

**9. Castilian villas nuevas (12th–14th c.): small blocks, narrow streets.** Sáinz Guerra (2014)
measured block widths in drone surveys and the cadastre. "La anchura de 25 metros es una cifra
recurrente … equivalentes a 30 varas o a 90 pies." "The 25-metre width is a recurring figure …
equal to 30 varas or 90 feet" (translation: Claude). Streets of 4 to 5 varas (3.3 to 4.2 m) are
common. Even in very regular towns the measures vary by up to about 5%. In the late Los Almudines
quarter of Alcañiz the lots are 5 × 15 varas (4.18 × 12.54 m), six to a short block face.

**10. Tenochtitlan (1519): an island city of house compounds.** Ana Rita Valero de García
Lascuráin (1990) writes that residential areas were "pequeñas, con superficies que iban desde los
100 a los 500 metros cuadrados", each holding one or more small houses facing an inner patio, not
the street. "Small, with areas from 100 to 500 square metres" (translation: Claude). Land belonged
to the calpulli (ward), which assigned house plots. The city grew from 180 ha to about 750 ha
through built-up chinampas (raised plots in the lake).

### What this adds to the English-language research

- **The law's real content.** English summaries often credit the 1573 Ordinances with a grid of
  fixed blocks and street widths. The Spanish text fixes only the plaza size and ratio, the eight
  streets out of the plaza, the arcades, and two lot sizes. Block size and street width were local
  choices.
- **Measured block sizes across Spanish America** (Puebla, Mexico City, Santiago, Lima, Buenos
  Aires) with their varas. Blocks are 2 to 8 times wider than Castilian medieval blocks.
- **A second Iberian lot type.** The Portuguese "lote estreito e comprido" with rua/travessa
  hierarchy, measured in palmos, is a distinct model from both the Spanish courtyard grid and the
  Islamic courtyard cluster. It is close to the northern European burgage plot, but Portuguese
  scholars tie it to steep sites and to the upper/lower town split.
- **Islamic town form as a process.** Spanish archaeology (CSIC Granada) treats the twisting lanes
  and adarves as the result of saturation over time, not a fixed "Islamic" layout. New excavations
  show planned orthogonal Islamic suburbs too. This undercuts a simple "Islamic = organic" rule.
- **Christian new towns measured from the cadastre**, with block widths of 25, 45 and 59 m and lot
  modules from founding charters.
- **Pre-Columbian numbers from Mexican and Peruvian scholars**: Teotihuacan compound sizes and its
  84 cm unit, Tenochtitlan plot sizes and area growth, Chan Chan extents.

### What this means for a generator and rule packs

- **A "Spanish colonial" rule pack is a plaza rule plus a local grid.** Hard part: plaza ratio of
  at least 1.5 : 1, size from 600 × 400 pies (medium) to 800 × 530 pies (maximum), 4 mid-side + 8
  corner streets, church on its own block near but not on the plaza (inland). Grid parameters are a
  per-city choice, sampled from the measured range: blocks of 115 to 210 m, streets of 9.5 to
  13.3 m, 4 or 8 lots per block.
- **Model densification as a time step, not a style.** Both the colonial sources (quarter-block
  lots split, Buenos Aires 1744) and the Andalusi sources (dispersed to saturated medina) describe
  the same thing: a sparse first layout that fills in, subdivides, and grows dead-end lanes. A rule
  pack can expose an "age" or "saturation" parameter that drives lot splitting and adarve creation.
- **A Portuguese rule pack needs a long narrow lot and a two-level town.** Lot about 6.6 × 13.2 m
  (30 × 60 palmos) or 5.3 × 11 m; ruas carry the fronts, travessas are short cross lanes; streets
  follow contours. On a coastal hill, split the town into an upper core (castle, cathedral,
  government) and a lower port strip linked by a few steep slopes.
- **An "Andalusi" rule pack: courtyard houses of about 90 m², secondary streets 2 to 3 m, tertiary
  lanes 1.4 to 1.6 m, adarves as branches that serve inner plots.** Optionally (Jeffrey decides)
  allow a planned-suburb variant with straight streets and planned dead ends.
- **A "Castilian new town" pack: blocks 25 to 59 m wide, streets 3.3 to 4.2 m, main street about
  6.7 m, long lots of about 4 to 7 m frontage and 12 to 25 m depth.** Real measures vary up to
  about 5% within one town, which fits a small deterministic jitter.
- **Units belong in the rule data.** Sources work in varas, pies, palmos and braças, with regional
  values (vara 0.768 to 0.837 m). A rule pack can state its unit and factor once, so numbers are
  copied from sources unchanged.
