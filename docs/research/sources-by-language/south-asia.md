## South Asian sources on town form

Scope: Sanskrit treatises (Arthashastra, Manasara, Mayamata, Samarangana Sutradhara), Tamil and Hindi web sources, Indian-published scholarship (B. B. Dutt 1925, the Tanjore *Vishvakarma Vastushastram* 1958, SRM Institute 2025), and Indian survey records (district gazetteers built on the 1872 and 1881 censuses). Every number below was read on a fetched page or a downloaded text unless it is marked "(unverified)".

Jargon used below:
- **Vastu / shilpa shastra**: Sanskrit manuals of building and town layout.
- **Danda** ("rod"), **hasta** or **aratni** or **kara** ("cubit", elbow to fingertip), **angula** ("finger-breadth"), **dhanus** or **capa** ("bow"): length units.
- **Pada**: one square cell of the square grid (mandala) that the treatises use to lay out a site.
- **Rajamarga**: royal road, the main street. **Rathya / vithi / marga**: lesser street classes.
- **Pol**: a gated neighbourhood in Ahmedabad. **Mohalla / muhalla**: a named urban quarter in north India. **Chaupar**: a square at a Jaipur crossroads. **Chowkri**: one of Jaipur's blocks.
- **Veedhi** (Tamil வீதி): street. **Mada veedhi**: a street ringing a temple. **Ther / ratha veedhi**: a car street, where the temple chariot is pulled.

### Findings

1. **The Arthashastra's road widths are 8 danda, not 4.** The GRETIL Sanskrit text groups the royal road with the roads to the larger towns, the countryside, pastures, camps, cremation grounds and villages under one word: *aṣṭa-daṇḍāḥ* (8 danda, about 13.5 m). The 1925 English summary by B. B. Dutt, following the Shamasastry translation, gives 4 danda for royal roads. That is a translation split, not the Sanskrit.
2. **The Arthashastra fort-city is a 4 x 4 ward grid with 12 gates.** It has three east–west and three north–south royal roads (KAZ 2.4.1) and twelve gates (2.4.2). Every compass sector gets named occupants (2.4.7–17). There is **one well per ten households** (2.4.26), towers every 30 danda (about 51 m, 2.3.10), and three moats 14, 12 and 10 danda wide (2.3.4).
3. **The Manasara gives eight named village plans, each with a size range in rods.** It also gives street-width ranges and quarter assignments by caste and trade, round by round on a concentric pada grid. Examples: Dandaka is 25–125 rods wide with a length of twice the breadth. Streets (vithi) are 3–12 rods (about 5.5–23 m). The main road is 9 rods (about 17 m). Houses on the main street are 3 rods wide and 2–3 times as deep.
4. **The Samarangana Sutradhara (ch. 10) gives a full set of numbers for three city sizes.** City breadth is 4000, 2000 or 1000 *capa* (bows). The royal road is 24, 20 or 16 hasta (about 10.2, 8.5 or 6.8 m). The two great streets are 12, 10 or 8 hasta. Four carriage roads are 4 hasta. Footpaths are 3, 2.5 or 2 hasta. Walls are 13–17 hasta high, and an even number of hasta is forbidden. There are three gates per side, and the main gates are 9, 8 or 7 hasta wide. The plan is a 64-pada grid with "16 blocks, 6 roads, 9 squares".
5. **The Mayamata (ch. 10) allows 13 street widths, from 1 to 7 danda in half-danda steps.** It sizes small towns at 100–300 danda (in steps of 10) and larger towns from 300 danda upward (in steps of 100).
6. **Later treatises fix the number of streets.** The Rajavallabha, quoted by Dutt, gives the best city 17 long streets and 17 cross streets, a middling city 13 each way, and the smallest real city 9. The Mayamata, quoted by Dutt, allows 1–12 streets each way, odd or even.
7. **Measured 19th-century densities are much lower than modern Indian cores.** Ahmedabad 1872: 116,873 people in 35,284 houses, about 3.3 people per house. Its divisions ran from 47 to 141 people per acre (116–349 per ha). Benares 1901: 209,331 people on 6,162 acres, 34 per acre (84 per ha) gross.
8. **Ahmedabad's pols are a counted, sized unit.** There were 356 pols, of which 275 were main pols and 81 sub-pols. Each had 5–60 houses, one or two gates closed at night, one main street with crooked side lanes, a common fund for "the pol gate, the pol privies, or the pol well", and a gatekeeper. One large pol was about 50 acres with 10,000 people.
9. **Tamil temple towns:** sources confirm the ring order and the social gradient, but I found no measured widths. Madurai's rings run Adi, Chithirai, Avani-Moola, Masi, then Veli on the line of the old fort. Upper castes lived nearest the temple and the poor farther out. Srirangam's seven walls total 32,592 ft (9,934 m), and its outer enclosures are residential.
10. **Jaipur and Shahjahanabad have only a few verified numbers.** Jaipur: 9 blocks (2 for palace and state), main streets 111 ft (34 m), 7 gates, and a wall about 6 m high and 3 m thick. Delhi: the Chandni Chowk is 40 yards wide and 1,520 yards long with 1,560 shops (Wikipedia). The 1883–84 Delhi Gazetteer says "about 50 yards wide" and about ¾ mile long. The city wall circuit is about 5½ miles with 10 gates.

### Case studies

1. **Arthashastra fort-capital (text).** A three-by-three road grid gives 16 wards and 12 gates. The palace sits in the ninth part north of centre. Each compass sector is given to a varna (the four classical social orders) and set of trades: Brahmins and metalworkers north, Kshatriyas and perfumers east, Vaishyas and food and liquor sellers south, Shudras and leather and weapon workers west. Guilds fill the leftover gaps. Shrines stand at the centre and groves 100 dhanus outside the moat. Cremation grounds lie north or east for the higher varnas and south for the lower, with outcasts at their edge. There is a well for every ten households.
2. **Manasara Nandyavarta village (text).** This is a square or oblong on a 64-, 81- or 49-pada grid, in rounds. The ring streets run clockwise: the eastern one north to south, the southern east to west, and so on. Seven, five, three or one wide vithis carry footpaths. Brahmins live in the inner rounds and Vaisyas and Sudras in the outer (Paisacha) round. Trades are placed by round: oilmen and potters on the east street, fishmongers and butchers west, hunters south. Temples of Vishnu face the village and Shiva temples turn their back on it.
3. **Samarangana Sutradhara city (text).** This is the most numerical treatise read. It covers three city classes with a fixed street hierarchy: royal road, two great streets, four carriage roads, half-width streets, and footpaths. Wall height has an odd-hasta-only rule, and there are 12 gates of set widths. It is directly usable as parameter tables.
4. **Ahmedabad, 1872–79 (measured).** About 117,000 people lived inside a 9.4 km wall with 18 gates, in 356 pols. Density doubled to tripled from the open western divisions (≈47/acre) to the packed eastern ones (≈141/acre). Each pol was a self-governing cul-de-sac cluster with one main street, crooked side lanes, a gate shut at night, a watchman, a well and privies. Drinking water came mainly from roof-fed courtyard cisterns.
5. **Benares, 1901 (measured).** It was 34 people per acre gross over the municipality. The ridge-top "pakka mahal" was dense masonry with stone-paved alleys. The low inland part had tanks, gardens and broad roads; many of its tanks were being filled in. The city was split into seven police wards, with a *muhalladar* answerable for each muhalla from 1803.
6. **Shahjahanabad, 1650–1883 (measured).** The 5½-mile wall had 10 gates. The fort sits mid-river-front. Two axial bazaars (Chandni Chowk to the Lahore gate, Faiz Bazar to the Delhi gate) are about 40–50 yards wide, with a central canal and later tree rows. Behind them are "narrow and tortuous" streets ending "in culs-de-sac". After 1857 a 300-yard glacis (open ground kept clear for defence) was cleared round the fort.
7. **Jaipur, 1727.** A 9-block plan with 2 blocks for the palace and state and 7 walled gates. Main streets are 111 ft (34 m), with chaupars at the crossings. Planner: Vidyadhar Bhattacharya, "प्राचीन भारतीय शिल्पशास्त्र के आधार पर" ("on the basis of ancient Indian shilpa shastra", translation: Claude), per Hindi Wikipedia. Block and chaupar sizes are unverified.
8. **Madurai.** "நகரமானது பொது மையத்தைக் கொண்ட நான்கு நாற்கர வடிவமுடைய தெருக்களாகப் பிரிக்கப்பட்டுள்ளது" (Tamil Wikipedia "மதுரை") = "The city is divided into four rectangular [rings of] streets sharing a common centre" (translation: Claude). The names are Adi, Chithirai, Avani-Moola and Masi, after the Tamil festival months. The Veli streets were laid on the line of the demolished fort. The date of the demolition is given as 1837 on Tamil Wikipedia and 1841 in a search snippet (unverified). "ஏழை மற்றும் பிற்பட்ட படிநிலை மக்கள் தொலைவிலுள்ள தெருக்களிலும் குடியிருந்தனர்" = "Poor and lower-ranked people lived in the more distant streets" (translation: Claude). Old literature compares the plan to a lotus and its petals.
9. **Thiruvarur.** The four car streets (*therveedhi* / *rathaveethi*) are named by direction, with mada streets (*madavilagam*) inside them and the Kamalalayam tank to the west. The chariot is about 96 ft high and about 350 t, and is built over eight weeks. Source: Vandana Balakrishnan & Narayana K. A., *Civil Engineering and Architecture* 13(2), 2025 (SRM Institute). That paper mislabels Vadakku as West and Merku as North; in Tamil these words mean North and West. No street widths are given.
10. **Srirangam.** Seven concentric walls total 9.9 km over 63 ha. The outer enclosures hold housing and markets, and the inner ones the shrines. No widths were found.

### What this adds to the English-language research

- **Primary numbers in the original language.** The Arthashastra 8-danda road reading comes straight from the Sanskrit. The widely repeated English figure of 4 danda for royal roads is a translation artefact.
- **Three more treatises with explicit parameter tables:** Manasara, Mayamata and Samarangana. They give size ranges, step increments, street hierarchies, gate counts and widths, and wall heights. These are rule-shaped data, unlike the descriptive European sources.
- **Social zoning given as an explicit direction × ring table** (Arthashastra by compass sector; Manasara by concentric round). This is a pattern the European corpus lacks: occupation and caste assigned by direction from the centre.
- **A whole settlement made of gated sub-units** (Ahmedabad pols). It is counted (356), sized (5–60 houses), and has its own institutions: gate, well, privies, fund, watchman.
- **Measured 19th-century Indian densities** (34–141 per acre; 84–349 per ha) and household sizes (≈3.0–3.35 per house in 1872 Ahmedabad) from Indian survey records.
- **Water rules.** One well per ten households (Arthashastra), tanks in the south, west or south-west (Manasara), and roof-fed courtyard cisterns as the main supply (Ahmedabad).

### What this means for a generator and rule packs

- **An "Indic shastra" rule pack can be pure data.** It needs: a city class (3 sizes in Samarangana; 8 village shapes in Manasara), a breadth range with a step, an aspect ratio (1, 1.125, 1.25, 1.5, 1.75 or 2), a pada grid (49, 64 or 81), a street-class table with widths, gates per side and gate widths, wall height and width, and moat count and widths. Each value maps onto a closed constraint kind (ADR-017) without a new evaluator.
- **Zoning by direction and ring is the key new constraint kind.** It is "occupant class X in compass sector S and/or ring R". Both the Arthashastra (sector) and Manasara (ring and sector) tables can be encoded as lookup data.
- **Gated sub-quarters (pols, mohallas, katras) are best modelled as a block type.** Its parameters: one or two gates, one spine street, branching dead-end lanes, a well, and 5–60 houses. A density parameter can then range from about 50 to about 140 people per acre.
- **Water density rule:** one well per 10 households (≈30–35 people at Ahmedabad's 3.3 per house), plus tanks placed by direction. Stepwells are rare landmarks (Dada Harir: 60 x 12 m), not routine.
- **Temple towns are concentric-ring generators.** They have 4–5 square rings named by festival month and an outermost ring on a demolished wall line. Status falls with distance from the temple, and car streets carry the processions. Widths are not documented in what I found, so they stay a GM? question or an explicit generator default.
- **Unit handling:** store everything in hasta or danda in the rule pack, with one conversion factor per pack (angula = 1.763 cm by default). Expose the ±15 % angula uncertainty as a pack parameter rather than baking it in.
